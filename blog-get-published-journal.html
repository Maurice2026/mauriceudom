How to Get Published in an Academic Journal

By Maurice Udom  ·  Step-by-Step Guide

Back to mauriceudom.org

Getting published in an academic journal is one of the most significant milestones in a scholar's career. It represents the formal recognition of your research by peers in your field, and it contributes your findings to the permanent record of human knowledge. Yet the process can seem opaque, intimidating, and — when rejection arrives — demoralising.

This guide demystifies the process. Whether you are a postgraduate student preparing your first submission or an established academic looking to sharpen your publishing strategy, these steps will help you navigate the journey from manuscript to publication.

Step 1: Know What You Have to Offer

1
Assess the originality and significance of your work
Before submitting anywhere, ask yourself honestly: What is the original contribution of this paper? What does it add to existing scholarship that was not there before? Academic journals are not interested in summaries of existing literature or competent rehearsals of well-established arguments. They want original findings, novel frameworks, new data, or significant reinterpretations of existing evidence. If you cannot articulate your contribution in two sentences, keep working on the paper.

Step 2: Choose the Right Journal

Journal selection is the most underestimated step in the publication process. Many manuscripts are rejected not because they are poor scholarship but because they are submitted to the wrong journal.

2a
Match your topic to the journal's scope
Read the journal's aims and scope carefully. Journals are often more specialised than their titles suggest. A journal of "African Studies" may in practice focus primarily on political science and economics, making it a poor fit for a paper on literary criticism.
2b
Assess the journal's prestige and indexing
Check whether the journal is indexed in major databases (Scopus, Web of Science, AJOL for African scholarship). Indexed journals carry more weight for promotions and grant applications. Also check the journal's impact factor and acceptance rate if available.
⚠️ Predatory Journals

Beware of predatory journals that charge publication fees and conduct little or no genuine peer review. If a journal emails you unsolicited invitations to submit, guarantees acceptance, or has an implausibly short review timeline, investigate it thoroughly at Beall's List or by consulting your institution's librarian before submitting.

Step 3: Prepare Your Manuscript

3a
Follow the journal's author guidelines exactly
Every journal publishes detailed submission guidelines covering word count, referencing style, abstract length, heading formats, and file types. Editors reject manuscripts at the desk — without sending them to peer review — simply because authors have not followed these guidelines. Download the guidelines and follow them precisely.
3b
Write a strong abstract
The abstract is often the first and sometimes only thing an editor reads before deciding whether to send your paper for review. It should clearly state your research question, methodology, key findings, and the significance of your contribution — all within the word limit (usually 150–250 words).
3c
Edit ruthlessly before submitting
A manuscript with grammatical errors, inconsistent referencing, or unclear prose signals to reviewers that the author has not taken the work seriously. Have a colleague or professional editor review the manuscript before submission. This is not vanity — it is professionalism.

Step 4: Submit and Wait

Most journals now use online submission systems (ScholarOne, Editorial Manager, OJS). Upload your files, complete the required metadata, write a brief cover letter addressed to the editor explaining why your paper is suitable for the journal, and submit.

"The cover letter is your paper's introduction to the editor. Keep it professional, specific, and brief — no more than three short paragraphs."

After submission, expect to wait. The peer review process typically takes between six weeks and six months, though some journals take longer. Most journals will acknowledge receipt and provide an estimated timeline. Do not submit the same paper to another journal while it is under review — simultaneous submission is considered unethical and can result in being blacklisted.

Step 5: Navigating Peer Review

Most submissions receive one of four decisions: Accept (rare on first submission), Minor Revisions, Major Revisions, or Reject. Accept the outcome professionally in all cases.

5a
Revisions — treat them as a gift
A request for revisions, even major ones, means the journal is interested in your work. Read the reviewers' comments carefully, multiple times. Prepare a detailed response document that addresses every single comment — even those you disagree with. Where you disagree, explain your reasoning respectfully and provide evidence.
5b
Rejection — treat it as information
Rejection is universal in academic publishing. The most celebrated scholars in every discipline have collected rejection letters throughout their careers. When rejected, read the reviewers' comments carefully. If they identify genuine weaknesses, address them. Then identify a new target journal and resubmit. Never abandon a paper after a single rejection.

Step 6: After Acceptance

Once accepted, the journal will send your paper through copyediting and typesetting. You will receive page proofs to check for errors. Review these carefully — this is your last opportunity to catch mistakes before the article enters the permanent scholarly record.

After publication, promote your work. Share the link on ResearchGate, Academia.edu, your institutional repository, and social media. Scholarship that is not read is scholarship that has not yet fulfilled its purpose.
