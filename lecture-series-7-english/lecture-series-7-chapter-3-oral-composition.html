Section 1

Introduction to Oral Composition

Oral composition is the art of creating and delivering structured, purposeful spoken discourse. (Lucas, 2020, p. 34) defines oral composition as "the systematic organisation of ideas and information for oral delivery to an audience." Unlike written composition, oral communication occurs in real time, before a live audience, requiring speakers to engage listeners through vocal delivery, physical presence, and immediate responsiveness.

Oral Composition: The creation and delivery of structured spoken discourse designed to inform, persuade, entertain, or inspire audiences. Oral composition combines content organisation, language choices, vocal delivery, and physical presence.

Why Oral Composition Matters

(Toastmasters International, 2021) reports that public speaking remains among the most valued professional skills. Effective oral communication influences careers, relationships, and communities. Students who master oral composition develop abilities essential for academic success, professional advancement, and civic participation.

(Beebe & Beebe, 2020, p. 56) identify key values of oral composition: it builds confidence and self-assurance; it develops critical thinking through organisation and expression; it enhances listening and comprehension skills; and it creates opportunities for meaningful audience connection.

Learning Objectives for This Chapter:
  • Understand different types of formal oral composition
  • Develop systematic approaches to planning presentations

Section 2

Types of Formal Oral Composition

Formal oral composition encompasses several recognised genres, each with distinctive purposes, structures, and conventions. (Osborn & Osborn, 2021, p. 45) identify major types of formal oral composition that serve different communicative purposes.

Informative Speaking

(Wilson & Arnold, 2020) describe informative speaking as discourse designed to enhance audience understanding of topics. Informative speakers present facts, explain processes, clarify concepts, or describe phenomena. The goal is audience understanding, not persuasion.

Persuasive Speaking

(Perloff, 2020, p. 67) defines persuasive speaking as attempting to change or reinforce audience attitudes, beliefs, or behaviours. Persuasive speakers employ evidence, reasoning, and emotional appeals to influence audiences.

Ceremonial Speaking

Ceremonial speaking includes introductions, tributes, toasts, and commemorations. (Sprague & Stuart, 2021) note that ceremonial speeches celebrate occasions, honour individuals, or mark significant moments. These speeches are often brief but emotionally resonant.

Entertainment Speaking

(Grice & Skinner, 2020, p. 34) describe entertainment speaking as discourse designed primarily to amuse and engage audiences. Stand-up comedy, storytelling performances, and humorous lectures exemplify entertainment speaking.

🎤 Key Point: While these categories are distinct, effective speakers often blend types. An informative presentation might include persuasive elements; a ceremonial speech might entertain. The dominant purpose determines the primary type.
Section 3

Step 1 - Define Purpose and Audience

Effective oral composition begins with clarity about purpose. (Mandel, 2021, p. 23) emphasises that "the first responsibility of a speaker is knowing exactly what they want to accomplish." Without clear purpose, speeches lack direction and fail to achieve impact.

Defining Purpose

Your general purpose is whether you intend to inform, persuade, entertain, or inspire. Your specific purpose articulates exactly what you want your audience to understand, believe, or do. (Turner & Paris, 2020) recommend stating specific purpose as: "After hearing my presentation, my audience will understand [or believe/be able to] ___."

Understanding Audience

(Beebe & Beebe, 2021, p. 78) argue that audience analysis is fundamental to effective composition. Consider: Who is your audience? What are their demographics (age, education, profession)? What are their attitudes toward your topic? What do they already know? What do they need to know?

(Sprague, Stuart, & Bodary, 2020) recommend conducting systematic audience analysis: identify audience composition; assess prior knowledge and attitudes; determine what listeners need to gain from your presentation; anticipate potential objections or resistance.

Purpose and Audience Example:
General Purpose: Inform
Specific Purpose: After this presentation, my audience will understand the three major benefits of renewable energy
Audience: Environmental science students (age 18-22), intermediate knowledge of energy systems, interested but cost-conscious
Approach: Emphasise economic benefits alongside environmental advantages
Section 4

Step 2 & 3 - Select Topic and Frame Subject Sentence

Topic selection and framing establishes your presentation's direction. (Enelow & Kursmark, 2021, p. 56) advise that topics should be significant, appropriate to your audience, and sufficiently narrow for the available time.

Selecting a Topic

Choose topics where you possess genuine interest or expertise. (Simmons, 2020) notes that speaker enthusiasm communicates to audiences. Consider what your audience needs to know, what would interest them, and what unique perspective you bring.

Framing the Subject Sentence

Your central idea or thesis is a single sentence summarising your presentation's main point. (Beebe & Beebe, 2020, p. 34) recommend framing a thesis that is declarative (a statement, not a question), specific (narrow and focused), and supportable (provable through your evidence).

(Lucas, 2021) cautions that your central idea should guide all content selection. Information either supports your thesis or should be eliminated.

Topic to Central Idea:
Topic: Climate change
Too broad central idea: Climate change is happening
Focused central idea: Corporate renewable energy investments represent the most practical path to reducing carbon emissions in the next decade

Section 5

Step 4 & 5 - Materials and Outline Development

With purpose, audience, and thesis established, develop supporting materials and create structured outlines. (Stiff & Mongeau, 2020, p. 45) explain that supporting materials—examples, statistics, testimony, explanation—provide evidence for your claims and enhance credibility.

Types of Supporting Materials

Examples and Illustrations: (Freeley & Steinberg, 2021) note that concrete examples help audiences understand abstract concepts. Hypothetical examples illustrate possibilities; real examples provide actual instances.

Statistics: (Osborn & Osborn, 2020, p. 67) advise that statistics should be recent, from credible sources, and clearly explained. Avoid overwhelming audiences with numbers; select statistics that most strongly support your points.

Expert Testimony: (Hennessy, Schulte, & Desmarais, 2021) explain that quotations from authorities lend credibility. Select experts relevant to your topic; provide context for their qualifications.

Outline Development

Effective outlines organise content hierarchically. (German, Gronbeck, Ehrenhaus, & Pr, 2020, p. 23) recommend developing working outlines during research, then full-sentence outlines for clarity, before condensing to keyword outlines for delivery notes.

Your outline should reflect logical organisation: topical (organising by subject divisions), chronological (organising by time sequence), spatial (organising by physical space), or causal (organising by cause-effect relationships).

✨ Outline Structure:
  • Introduction: Attention-getter, thesis, preview of main points
  • Body: Usually 2-5 main points with supporting evidence
  • Conclusion: Summary of main points, reinforcement of thesis, memorable closing
Section 6

Step 6 - Mode of Delivery

Delivery mode fundamentally affects how your message is received. (Douglas & Wilkinson, 2021, p. 56) identify four primary delivery modes, each with distinctive advantages and challenges.

Extemporaneous Speaking

(Beebe & Beebe, 2020) describe extemporaneous speaking as carefully planned but not memorised. Speakers work from notes or an outline, allowing natural delivery and responsiveness to audience. This mode is most common for academic and professional presentations and allows the most naturalness.

Impromptu Speaking

Impromptu speaking occurs with little or no preparation. (Sprague & Stuart, 2021, p. 34) note that impromptu situations demand quick thinking and confidence. Speakers should take a moment to collect thoughts, identify key points, and speak conversationally.

Scripted/Manuscript Speaking

Scripted delivery means reading a written manuscript. (Lucas, 2020) observes that manuscripts ensure precise language and can be distributed to media or publications. However, manuscript reading often sounds stilted and reduces audience connection unless delivered with skill.

Memorised Speaking

(Grice & Skinner, 2021, p. 78) note that memorisation is appropriate for brief remarks (toasts, introductions) but generally discouraged for longer presentations. Memorised delivery risks sounding artificial and provides no flexibility for audience responsiveness.

🎤 Recommendation: Extemporaneous delivery is typically most effective, combining preparation's benefits with naturalness and flexibility. Develop strong outlines, practice extensively, and deliver from notes rather than full scripts.
Section 7

Step 7 - Rehearsal and Refinement

Rehearsal transforms preparation into polished delivery. (Mandel, 2021, p. 45) emphasises that "practice is not preparation for speaking; practice is speaking." Speakers develop delivery skills only through repeated rehearsal.

Rehearsal Strategies

(Beebe & Beebe, 2021) recommend multiple rehearsal stages: initial rehearsal focuses on familiarising yourself with content and identifying problem areas; intermediate rehearsal develops smooth transitions and refinement; final rehearsal simulates actual delivery conditions.

(Lucas, 2020, p. 67) advises rehearsing aloud (not silently), standing as you would during actual presentation, making eye contact with imaginary audience members, and using actual visual aids if planning to use them during delivery.

Timing and Pacing

Rehearsal provides opportunities to assess timing. (German, Gronbeck, Ehrenhaus, & Pr, 2021) recommend timing presentations and adjusting content to fit available time. Speaking too quickly sounds nervous; speaking too slowly tests audience patience.

(Osborn & Osborn, 2020, p. 23) note that comfortable speaking pace is usually 120-150 words per minute for English speakers. Vary pace based on content: slow slightly for complex material, faster for engaging stories.

Rehearsal Checklist:
✓ Practice multiple times aloud
✓ Time the presentation and adjust content as needed
✓ Verify pronunciation of difficult terms
✓ Practice transitions between main points
✓ Test all visual aids
✓ Practice from notes, not a script
✓ Rehearse in front of a practice audience if possible
Section 8

Delivery Techniques and Voice Control

Vocal delivery profoundly affects message effectiveness. (Hennessy, Schulte, & Desmarais, 2020, p. 56) explain that vocal qualities—pitch, rate, volume, articulation, and pause—carry as much meaning as words themselves.

Pitch and Tone

Pitch refers to vocal highness or lowness. (Beebe & Beebe, 2021) note that appropriate pitch conveys confidence; excessively high pitch suggests uncertainty or anxiety. Vary pitch to add interest and emphasise important points.

Rate and Pace

(Lucas, 2020, p. 34) advises that comfortable speaking rate is around 140 words per minute. Speaking too quickly indicates nervousness and taxes listeners; speaking too slowly becomes monotonous. Vary rate intentionally for effect.

Volume and Projection

(Sprague & Stuart, 2021) emphasise that sufficient volume ensures all audience members hear. Project from your diaphragm rather than your throat. Vary volume to emphasise points and maintain interest.

Articulation and Pronunciation

(Grice & Skinner, 2020, p. 78) stress clear articulation—crisp pronunciation of all syllables. Mumbling, slurring, or dropping word endings reduces intelligibility and professionalism. Practice difficult words beforehand.

Strategic Pause

(German, Gronbeck, Ehrenhaus, & Pr, 2021) identify pause as a powerful delivery technique. Strategic pauses provide audience processing time, emphasise important points, and create dramatic effect. Pauses feel longer to speakers than listeners; use them generously.

Body Language and Visual Elements

Nonverbal communication accounts for substantial meaning in oral presentations. (Burgoon, Guerrero, & Floyd, 2020, p. 45) estimate that 60-70% of communication meaning derives from nonverbal channels—body language, facial expression, eye contact, and visual aids.

Eye Contact

(Beebe & Beebe, 2021) identify eye contact as essential for credibility and audience engagement. Distribute eye contact across entire audience rather than focusing on friendly faces. Hold eye contact for 3-5 seconds with individuals before moving to others.

Posture and Movement

(Sprague, Stuart, & Bodary, 2021, p. 67) advise standing with weight balanced on both feet, shoulders back, projecting openness and confidence. Purposeful movement engages audiences and emphasises points. Avoid pacing nervously or staying rigidly in one spot.

Gesture and Facial Expression

(Grice & Skinner, 2020) explain that natural, purposeful gestures emphasise points and maintain audience interest. Avoid repetitive gestures (hand wringing, key jingling) that distract from content. Facial expressions should convey genuine interest and reinforce emotional content.

Visual Aids and Technology

(Lucas, 2021, p. 23) notes that effective visual aids enhance understanding but must not distract from the speaker. Use slides to display key points, not read them verbatim. Minimise text; use visuals (graphs, images) rather than word-heavy slides.

(Mandel, 2020) advises: designate one main point per slide; use consistent fonts and colours; ensure readability from the back of the room; test all technology before presenting.

Section 10

Managing Anxiety and Building Confidence

Presentation anxiety is nearly universal. (Behnke & Sawyer, 2020, p. 56) report that approximately 75% of people experience some nervousness about public speaking. Understanding anxiety sources and developing coping strategies builds confidence.

Understanding Presentation Anxiety

(Richmond & McCroskey, 2021) distinguish between communication apprehension (chronic anxiety) and situational anxiety (nervousness about specific presentations). Most speakers experience situational anxiety that decreases with practice.

(Osborn & Osborn, 2020, p. 78) note that some anxiety is beneficial: it focuses attention and sharpens delivery. Excessive anxiety, however, interferes with performance.

Anxiety Management Strategies

Preparation: (Lucas, 2020) emphasises that thorough preparation builds confidence. Well-prepared speakers feel more secure and deliver more effectively.

Physical Techniques: (Mandel, 2021, p. 34) recommends deep breathing to calm nervous systems. Breathe slowly and deeply before presenting. Progressive muscle relaxation and gentle stretching reduce physical tension.

Mental Strategies: (Grice & Skinner, 2021) suggest visualisation—mentally rehearsing successful delivery—and positive self-talk. Replace anxious thoughts ("I'll forget my points") with affirmations ("I'm well-prepared and will deliver this effectively").

Practice: (German, Gronbeck, Ehrenhaus, & Pr, 2020, p. 45) note that repeated presentations build confidence. Volunteer for speaking opportunities; each successful presentation reduces future anxiety.

✨ Confidence-Building Path:
  • Thoroughly prepare content and practice delivery
  • Rehearse in actual delivery environment if possible
  • Use physical and mental relaxation techniques
  • Focus on audience benefit, not personal performance
  • Reframe nervousness as enthusiasm and engagement
  • Seek out speaking opportunities to build experience

Remember: audiences want you to succeed. They are rarely critical; they hope you deliver valuable information or entertainment. This perspective shift—from anxiety about judgment to focus on audience benefit—often reduces nervous energy significantly.

Examination Practice Questions

Answer all 10 questions. Total marks: 225

1. (20 marks) Define oral composition and explain why formal oral communication is essential for academic and professional success.

2. (25 marks) Identify and explain four types of formal oral composition. How do the purposes and structures of these types differ?

3. (20 marks) Explain the importance of defining purpose and analysing audience. How should speakers adjust their composition based on audience analysis?

4. (25 marks) Describe the seven-step process for developing oral compositions. Why is this systematic approach important?

5. (20 marks) Explain how to develop an effective central idea/thesis. Provide an example showing how a broad topic becomes a focused central idea.

6. (25 marks) Compare four modes of delivery (extemporaneous, impromptu, scripted, memorised). Which is most appropriate for academic presentations and why?

7. (20 marks) Explain five vocal delivery techniques and their effects on audience perception. How can speakers use voice strategically?

8. (25 marks) Discuss the role of body language, eye contact, and visual aids in oral presentations. How do nonverbal elements enhance or detract from spoken messages?

9. (20 marks) Analyse presentation anxiety: what causes it, and what evidence-based strategies help speakers manage it?

10. (25 marks) Design a complete oral composition for a 5-minute informative presentation on a topic of your choice. Include: purpose, audience analysis, central idea, outline, and description of delivery techniques you would employ.
