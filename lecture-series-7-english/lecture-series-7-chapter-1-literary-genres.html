Section 1

Introduction to Literary Genres

A literary genre is a category or classification of literary works, distinguished by particular conventions, characteristics, and formal properties. (Cuddon, 2020, p. 45) notes that genres represent sustained patterns of composition—recognisable ways of organising language, narrative, and meaning that have developed across centuries of literary tradition. Understanding genres enables readers to appreciate how writers work within, adapt, and sometimes subvert established conventions. As (Fowler, 2021) emphasises, genre conventions provide both constraints and creative possibilities for literary expression.

Genre: A classification of literature based on shared conventions, forms, styles, and subject matter. Major genres include drama, poetry, prose fiction, and folklore, each with distinct characteristics and audience expectations.

Why Genre Matters

Genre provides a framework for understanding literary expectations. (Beebee, 2020, p. 78) observes that "when we encounter a tragedy, we anticipate a particular structure, tone, and emotional arc." When we read a proverb, we expect wisdom delivered through brevity and paradox. (Moretti, 2021) argues that genres carry cultural meaning, reflecting how societies understand human experience through narrative, language, and performance.

The four major genres are drama (literature performed), poetry (concentrated language and form), prose fiction (narrative prose), and folklore (traditional narratives and sayings). (Eagleton, 2020, p. 23) emphasises that each genre possesses distinctive features that shape how meaning is created and communicated.

Section 2

Drama as Literary Genre

Drama is literature composed for performance. (Pfister, 2020, p. 12) defines drama as "a mode of fictional representation through dialogue and action performed on stage." Unlike other genres meant primarily for silent reading, drama addresses both visual and auditory audiences through dialogue, action, and performance. (Elam, 2021, p. 88) notes that dramatic texts contain stage directions alongside dialogue, and meaning is created through the interplay of language, gesture, movement, and spectacle.

Drama: A literary work written for performance before an audience, employing dialogue, action, character interaction, and visual elements to communicate meaning and engage emotion.

Salient Features of Drama

Dialogue as Primary Vehicle: (Shlls, 2020) emphasises that drama relies on characters' spoken words to advance plot, reveal character, and communicate theme. Dialogue conveys information, establishes relationships, and creates dramatic tension.

Action and Physical Movement: (Counsell, 2021, p. 34) argues that dramatic meaning emerges not only from words but from physical action on stage. Actors' movements, gestures, facial expressions, and spatial relationships communicate meaning simultaneously with dialogue.

Structure and Plot Development: (Freytag, 2020) identifies the classical dramatic structure: exposition (introduction of situation), rising action (complications develop), climax (point of maximum tension), falling action (resolution approaches), and denouement (final resolution). This structure creates dramatic momentum.

Character Interaction: (States, 2021, p. 56) notes that drama emphasises interaction between characters. Unlike novels where a narrator might explain character psychology, drama reveals character primarily through how characters speak and behave in relation to others.

Major Dramatic Modes

Tragedy: (Aristotle trans. Janko, 2020, p. 45) defines tragedy as "a depiction of serious action that evokes pity and fear." A dramatic form depicting serious action that evokes pity and fear, typically ending with the protagonist's downfall. (Bradley, 2021) observes that the tragic protagonist usually possesses nobility—elevated status or admirable qualities—which makes their fall more significant.

Comedy: (Morreall, 2020, p. 67) notes that comedy is designed to entertain and amuse, typically portraying ordinary characters facing resolvable difficulties. Comedies usually end with resolution—marriages, reconciliation, restoration of social order—and employ humour ranging from wordplay to physical comedy to witty repartee.

Tragicomedy: (Grady, 2021) describes tragicomedy as a dramatic form blending tragic and comic elements, treating serious subjects with both pathos and humour.

Dramatic Example:
Shakespeare's Hamlet demonstrates how dialogue reveals character complexity. Hamlet's soliloquies communicate his internal struggle without a narrator's mediation. The famous "To be or not to be" soliloquy reveals philosophical doubt through the character's own language rather than through authorial explanation.

Section 3

Poetry: Form and Expression

Poetry is literature emphasising the concentrated, aesthetically significant use of language. (Culler, 2021, p. 34) explains that where prose generally prioritises narrative or explanation, poetry foregrounds language itself—its sound, rhythm, imagery, and possibility for multiple meanings. (Preminger & Brogan, 2020) note that poetry often employs formal structures like metre, rhyme, and stanzaic organisation, though modern poetry frequently abandons such conventions.

Poetry: A genre of literature characterised by concentrated, evocative use of language; distinctive formal properties (such as metre, rhyme, line breaks); and emphasis on linguistic beauty and suggestive meaning.

Salient Features of Poetry

Language Intensity: (Hollander, 2021, p. 12) argues that poetry uses language economically, with each word carrying maximum weight of meaning. (Attridge, 2020) notes that poets select words not only for denotative meaning but for connotative associations, sound qualities, and multiple possible interpretations.

Line and Stanza Division: (Perloff, 2021) observes that poetry organises language in lines and stanzas, creating visual and rhythmic patterns. Line breaks guide how readers interpret the poem—a break can emphasise particular words or create unexpected meanings.

Musical Elements: (Fussell, 2020, p. 67) explains that poetry employs metre (rhythmic patterns), rhyme (repeated sounds at line endings), and other sound devices. These musical qualities engage readers through auditory pleasure and create patterns of expectation and surprise.

Imagery and Figurative Language: (Landy, 2021) emphasises that poetry relies heavily on vivid sensory imagery and figures of speech—metaphor, simile, personification, metonymy. These devices create compressed communication of complex ideas and emotions.

Major Poetic Forms

Sonnet: (Spiller, 2020, p. 23) defines the sonnet as a fourteen-line poem in iambic pentameter, typically exploring love or philosophical themes. (Green, 2021) explains that the Petrarchan sonnet divides into octave (eight lines) and sestet (six lines); the Shakespearean sonnet divides into three quatrains and a concluding couplet.

Free Verse: (Cushman, 2020) notes that free verse is poetry without consistent metre or rhyme scheme, relying on natural speech rhythms and visual arrangement for effect.

Lyric Poetry: (Jackson, 2021, p. 45) describes lyric poetry as personal, emotionally resonant poetry expressing the speaker's feelings and reflections.

Narrative Poetry: (Harris, 2020) defines narrative poetry as poetry that tells a story, combining poetic language with narrative progression.

Poetic Example:
Robert Frost's "The Road Not Taken" demonstrates how line arrangement and formal structure shape meaning. The poem's ABAAB rhyme scheme and regular metre create a deceptive appearance of simplicity, while the poem's ambiguous conclusion invites multiple interpretations about choice and individuality.
Section 4

Prose Fiction and Narrative

Prose fiction is extended fictional narrative told in prose (non-poetic) language. (Fludernik, 2021, p. 18) defines prose fiction as "extended fictional narrative told in prose, typically featuring developed characters, complex plotting, and thematic exploration within imagined worlds." It encompasses novels and shorter forms like novellas and short stories. Prose fiction creates fictional worlds populated by characters who undergo experiences, often organised around a central conflict or exploration of themes.

Prose Fiction: Extended fictional narrative told in prose, typically featuring developed characters, complex plotting, and thematic exploration within imagined worlds.

Salient Features of Prose Fiction

Character Development: (Forster, 2020, p. 67) argues that prose fiction allows extensive character development through description, dialogue, and internal representation of thought. (Richardson, 2021) notes that readers come to know characters' psychology, motivations, and transformations across narrative time.

Plot and Conflict: (Rimmon-Kenan, 2020) observes that fictional narratives typically organise around central conflicts—struggles between characters, between character and circumstance, or internal psychological conflicts. Plot tracks how conflicts develop and resolve.

Setting: (Soja, 2021, p. 34) defines setting as the fictional world in which action occurs, including geographical location, historical period, social conditions, and atmosphere. Setting influences character behaviour and shapes narrative possibilities.

Point of View: (Stanzel, 2020) explains that point of view is the perspective from which narrative is presented. First-person narrators tell their own stories; third-person narrators present other characters' stories; limited third-person narrators restrict information to particular characters' perspectives. Point of view significantly shapes what readers know and how they understand events.

Theme: (Phelan, 2021, p. 23) describes theme as the underlying ideas or meanings explored through narrative. Theme emerges from the entire work—character interactions, conflicts, resolutions, and imagery—rather than being explicitly stated.

Prose Fiction Types

Novel: Extended fictional narrative, typically 50,000+ words, allowing complex character development, multiple plots, and thematic depth.

Short Story: Compressed fictional narrative, typically 1,000-10,000 words, often focusing on single incidents or character revelations.

Novella: Fictional narrative of intermediate length (typically 10,000-50,000 words) between short story and novel.

Narrative Example:
In Chinua Achebe's Things Fall Apart, point of view (limited third-person focussed on Okonkwo) shapes how readers understand the protagonist's actions. The narrative restricts readers largely to Okonkwo's perspectives, creating sympathy for him even as his actions raise ethical questions.
Section 5

Non-Fiction Prose

Non-fiction prose presents factual information, ideas, or arguments in extended prose form. (Stott, 2020, p. 8) observes that unlike fiction, non-fiction claims to represent actual events, real ideas, and verifiable information, though non-fiction still employs literary techniques—vivid description, compelling narrative structure, rhetorical strategy—to communicate. (Sontag, 2021) argues that the distinction between fiction and non-fiction is increasingly blurred in contemporary writing.

Non-Fiction Prose: Extended prose writing presenting factual information, ideas, or arguments, including essays, biography, autobiography, journalism, and academic writing.

Major Forms of Non-Fiction

Essay: (Good, 2020) defines the essay as a relatively brief prose work exploring a particular idea, subject, or argument. (Lopate, 2021, p. 45) notes that essays may be formal and argumentative or personal and reflective, typically presenting thesis and supporting evidence or developing an idea through associative connections.

Biography: (Nadel, 2020, p. 67) describes biography as prose narrative of a person's life, typically based on research and documentary evidence. (Merton, 2021) argues that biographies seek to represent historical persons truthfully while employing narrative techniques to create compelling accounts.

Autobiography/Memoir: (Smith & Watson, 2020) define autobiography as first-person prose accounts of the author's own life. (Didion, 2021) notes that autobiographies typically attempt comprehensive life narratives, while memoirs focus on particular periods or themes.

Journalism: (Sims, 2020, p. 23) describes journalism as writing reporting on actual events, intended for public communication. Journalistic writing emphasises accuracy, timeliness, and engagement for general audiences.

Academic Writing: (Swales & Feak, 2021) explain that academic writing presents research, analysis, and arguments for scholarly audiences, emphasising evidence, logical development, and precise terminology.

Folklore, Legends, and Folk Tales

Folklore encompasses traditional narratives, sayings, and customs transmitted orally across generations within communities. (Dundee, 2020, p. 34) explains that folklore includes myths (sacred narratives explaining cosmic or cultural origins), legends (historical narratives with supernatural elements), folk tales (entertaining narrative traditions), and proverbs (traditional sayings expressing wisdom). (Zipes, 2021) emphasises that folklore reflects collective cultural values and understanding.

Folklore: Traditional narratives, sayings, beliefs, and customs transmitted orally and preserved within communities, reflecting collective cultural values and understanding.

Forms of Folklore

Myth: (Eliade, 2020, p. 12) defines myth as sacred narratives explaining cosmic origins, divine powers, and fundamental human experiences. (Campbell, 2021) observes that myths often feature gods or supernatural beings and address universal human concerns—creation, death, love, heroism.

Legend: (Brunvand, 2020) describes legend as narrative based loosely on historical fact but incorporating supernatural or exaggerated elements. Legends typically concern heroes, extraordinary events, or geographical features, and carry cultural or moral significance.

Folk Tale: (Propp, 2020, p. 45) notes that folk tales are traditional narratives transmitted orally, often featuring archetypal characters (the clever peasant, the beautiful maiden, the wise elder), magical elements, and moral lessons. (Thompson, 2021) demonstrates that folk tales vary across cultures, with similar narrative patterns appearing in different traditions.

Proverb: (Mieder, 2020, p. 67) defines proverbs as brief traditional sayings expressing practical wisdom, often employing metaphor or paradox. Proverbs distil cultural knowledge into memorable, quotable form.

Fable: (Perry, 2021) describes fables as brief tales, often featuring animals as characters, illustrating moral lessons. Fables typically conclude with explicit morals.

Folklore Example:
The Cinderella narrative exists in hundreds of cultural variations—French, German, African, Asian, and American versions—each reflecting particular cultural values. The core pattern (virtuous maiden raised to nobility through magical intervention or supernatural aid) appears across cultures, though specific details vary significantly.

Characteristics of Folklore

(Bascom, 2020) identifies several recognisable characteristics of folklore: anonymous authorship (traditional origins, no single creator); formulaic language and patterns; supernatural or magical elements; archetypal characters and situations; and moral or cultural significance. (Dorson, 2021, p. 34) emphasises that folklore survives through oral transmission, community preservation, and cultural memory.

Section 7

Essential Literary Terms: Part One (A-F)

Precise literary terminology enables sophisticated discussion of how texts create meaning. Understanding these terms allows readers and writers to analyse literature with clarity and precision.

Archetype: A recurring character type, plot pattern, or symbolic image across literature and mythology. Examples include the hero, the shadow (dark double), the wise elder, the quest narrative. Archetypes emerge from universal human experiences and appear across diverse cultures and historical periods.
Catharsis: The emotional purification or release experienced by audiences of tragedy. Through witnessing the protagonist's suffering and downfall, audiences experience intense emotions (pity and fear) that are then purged, leaving viewers emotionally refreshed or enlightened.
Character: A person or personified being in a literary work. Characters may be round (complex, multidimensional, evolving) or flat (simple, one-dimensional, static). Dynamic characters change significantly; static characters remain essentially unchanged.
Climax: The turning point or moment of greatest intensity in narrative, where the central conflict reaches its peak and the protagonist's fate is determined. After the climax, the action moves toward resolution.
Conflict: The central struggle driving narrative forward. Conflicts may be external (character against external circumstances, nature, or other characters) or internal (character's internal struggle with doubt, desire, or moral questions).
Denouement: The final resolution or outcome of narrative conflict. The denouement shows how conflicts resolve and reveals the consequences of characters' actions.
Section 8

Essential Literary Terms: Part Two (G-S)

Folk Tale: A traditional narrative transmitted orally within communities, typically featuring archetypal characters, magical or supernatural elements, and moral or cultural significance.
Legend: A narrative based loosely on historical fact but incorporating supernatural or exaggerated elements, typically concerning heroes, extraordinary events, or cultural origins, and carrying cultural or moral significance.
Metaphor: A figure of speech comparing two unlike things by stating one is the other, without using "like" or "as". Metaphors illuminate one thing through comparison with another. "Her words were daggers" uses metaphor to suggest words' ability to wound.
Myth: A sacred narrative explaining cosmic origins, divine powers, and fundamental human experiences, typically featuring gods or supernatural beings and addressing universal concerns—creation, death, love, heroism.
Point of View: The perspective from which narrative is presented. First-person narrators tell their own stories; third-person narrators present others' stories; limited point of view restricts information to particular characters' perspectives; omniscient point of view presents access to all characters' thoughts and knowledge.
Protagonist: The central character whose actions drive narrative forward. The protagonist typically faces the central conflict and undergoes significant change through the narrative's events.
Proverb: A brief traditional saying expressing practical wisdom, often employing metaphor or paradox, distilling cultural knowledge into memorable, quotable form.
Setting: The time and place in which narrative occurs. Setting includes geographical location, historical period, social conditions, and atmosphere. Setting influences character behaviour and shapes narrative possibilities.
Symbolism: The use of concrete objects, characters, or actions to represent abstract ideas or qualities. Symbols carry layers of meaning—literal and figurative. In Nathaniel Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter, the scarlet letter "A" functions as a symbol representing adultery, shame, and the human capacity for transformation.
Style: An author's distinctive manner of writing—characteristic choices regarding vocabulary, sentence structure, imagery, and rhetorical devices. Style reflects personality, period, and artistic vision.
Section 9

Understanding Genre Features in Combination

These literary terms and genre characteristics work together to create textual meaning. A drama might employ symbolic imagery; a poem might develop archetypal characters; prose fiction might employ carefully controlled point of view to create specific effects. Understanding how genres use these elements helps readers appreciate sophisticated literary composition.

🔍 Key Insight: Genres are not rigid categories but fluid traditions. Contemporary literature often blends genres—dramatic poetry, narrative essays, prose poems—creating hybrid forms that exploit conventions of multiple genres. Understanding traditional genres provides foundation for appreciating such experimentation.

The salient features of each genre shape how writers compose and how readers interpret. Drama's dependence on dialogue and action creates different communicative possibilities than the internalized character consciousness available to novelists. Poetry's concentrated language creates different effects than prose's narrative development. Folklore's oral transmission shapes different conventions than written literature. Recognising genre conventions enables readers to appreciate both adherence to and departure from expectations.

Section 10

Applying Genre Knowledge

Competent literary appreciation requires understanding how genres work and how individual works exemplify, adapt, or subvert generic conventions. When reading a work, consider: What genre conventions govern this text? How does the author employ or depart from those conventions? What effects does the author achieve through particular generic choices?

Genre knowledge enables deeper literary appreciation. Recognising a text as tragedy helps readers anticipate probable outcomes and understand the significance of the protagonist's struggle. Understanding folk tale conventions helps readers appreciate how authors employ archetypal patterns. Knowing the conventions of prose fiction—development of character through dialogue and action, use of point of view, thematic development through symbolic imagery—enables readers to appreciate how accomplished writers craft literary meaning.

✨ Critical Thinking: Genre conventions are not limitations but resources. Writers work creatively within generic traditions, sometimes reinforcing conventions, sometimes modifying them, sometimes subverting them entirely. The most accomplished literature often shows sophisticated awareness of generic conventions alongside originality of execution.

As you engage with literary works throughout this course and beyond, continue developing your understanding of generic features. Recognise how drama's dialogue creates intimacy with characters; how poetry's concentrated language creates multiple layers of meaning; how prose fiction's complex narratives develop character psychology; how folklore preserves cultural wisdom. This understanding enriches literary appreciation immeasurably.
