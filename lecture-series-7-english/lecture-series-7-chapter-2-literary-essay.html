Section 1

Introduction to Literary Essays

A literary essay is analytical prose that examines, interprets, and evaluates literary texts. (Griffith, 2020, p. 34) defines literary essays as "sustained arguments about textual meaning, grounded in close reading and supported by specific textual evidence." Unlike personal essays that prioritise the writer's experience or feelings, literary essays focus on understanding how texts create meaning through language, form, and artistic choices.

Literary Essay: Analytical prose that interprets and evaluates literary texts through careful textual examination, argumentation supported by evidence, and demonstration of how literary elements create meaning.

Purposes of Literary Essays

(Abrams & Harpham, 2020, p. 67) identifies several purposes literary essays serve: they deepen understanding of particular texts; they demonstrate critical thinking about literature; they communicate interpretations persuasively to readers; they participate in ongoing scholarly conversations about texts and meaning. Literary essays range from brief analytical responses to extended scholarly arguments.

Two fundamental approaches characterise literary essays: explication (close reading and detailed analysis of specific texts) and review (evaluation and critical judgment of works). This chapter addresses both, examining how to explicate novels, short stories, and poems, and how to write effective critical reviews.

Learning Objectives for This Chapter:
  • Explicate novels with attention to narrative technique and thematic development
  • Explicate short stories, attending to compressed narrative and symbolic density
  • Explicate poetry, examining form, language, and meaning
  • Write convincing literary reviews and critical evaluations
  • Apply critical analysis to dramatic and broadcast media
  • Construct arguments supported by textual evidence
Section 2

Explication Defined and Applied

Explication means "unfolding" or "unravelling"—explaining a text's layers of meaning through systematic, careful analysis. (Brooks & Warren, 2020, p. 45) explain that "explication is the patient untangling of the knot of meaning." Explication differs from summary: while summaries recount plot or content, explications examine how texts create meaning.

Explication: Detailed analysis of a text that unfolds and explains its layers of meaning, examining how literary elements—language, structure, imagery, characterisation, form—work together to create significance.

Principles of Explication

Textual Fidelity: (Wimsatt & Beardsley, 2021) emphasise that explication must remain faithful to the text itself, using specific textual evidence to support interpretations.

Systematic Analysis: (Cleanth Brooks, 2020, p. 89) argues that explication proceeds methodically, examining individual elements—words, images, rhythmic patterns, structural choices—and demonstrating how they contribute to overall meaning.

Recognition of Complexity: Good explication acknowledges that literary texts are complex, containing multiple meanings, tensions, and ambiguities. (Burke, 2021) notes that explication reveals this complexity rather than reducing texts to single meanings.

Attention to Form: (Preminger, 2020, p. 23) stresses that form and content are inseparable in literature. Explication must examine how formal choices—genre conventions, structure, language—shape meaning.

🔍 Key Principle: Explication is not invention of meaning but discovery of meaning created through textual artistry. The goal is to show how readers' experiences and understandings emerge from texts' formal and linguistic properties.
Section 3

Explicating Novels

Novels, extended fictional narratives, offer rich opportunities for explication. (Booth, 2021, p. 56) observes that novels develop meaning through narrative perspective, character development, plot structure, and thematic progression across extended length. Explicating novels requires attention to how these elements work across the entire text.

Key Elements in Novel Explication

Narrative Perspective and Voice: (Stanzel, 2020) argues that how a novel narrates—through first-person reflection, limited third-person consciousness, omniscient narration—profoundly shapes readers' experiences and understanding. Explication must examine how the narrator's perspective influences our comprehension of events and characters.

Character Development and Psychology: (Hochman, 2021, p. 34) explains that novels develop character through action, dialogue, internal thought, and external description. Characters may develop—changing and learning through experience—or remain static. Explication examines character complexity and psychological depth.

Plot Structure and Causality: (Forster, 2020) distinguishes plot (connected events with causal logic) from story (sequential events). Novel explication attends to how plots develop tension, create turning points, and resolve conflicts meaningfully.

Thematic Development: Novels explore themes—large ideas and concerns—through multiple scenes, characters, and incidents. (Phelan, 2021, p. 78) notes that explicit thematic statements matter less than how themes emerge through narrative action and characterisation.

Novel Explication Example:
In Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, explication might examine how Elizabeth Bennet's limited perspective (she often misinterprets others' motives) shapes readers' experience. The novel's third-person limited narration reveals Elizabeth's prejudices alongside her witty observations, forcing readers to appreciate her intelligence while recognising her limitations. This narrative technique embodies the novel's central theme: "It is a truth universally acknowledged" that first impressions deceive.
Section 4

Explicating Short Stories

Short stories, compressed narratives typically told in 5,000-15,000 words, require distinctive explication strategies. (May, 2020, p. 45) notes that short stories achieve power through compression and selectivity. Every element—each word, image, character detail—carries weight. Explication must attend to this symbolic density.

Distinctive Features of Short Story Explication

Symbolic Intensity: Short stories often employ symbolic objects or actions carrying significant meaning. (Lohafer, 2021) explains that short story symbols frequently operate simultaneously on literal and symbolic levels, demanding careful interpretation.

Epiphanic Moments: (Dubois, 2020, p. 67) observes that short stories frequently culminate in moments of revelation or understanding where characters (and readers) suddenly perceive significance previously overlooked. Explication must identify these epiphanic moments and explain their emotional and thematic weight.

Economy of Characterisation: Short stories reveal character efficiently, often through single revealing actions or details rather than extended development. (Hansbury, 2021) argues that short story characterisation demands sophisticated inference from minimal information.

Compressed Plot: Short story plots concentrate significant conflict and change into brief compass. (Wood, 2020, p. 34) notes that short story plots often begin in medias res (in the middle of action) and move swiftly to crisis and resolution.

Short Story Explication Example:
James Joyce's "Araby" describes a young boy's infatuation with his neighbour's sister. The story's climax occurs when the boy reaches a bazaar intending to purchase a gift, only to discover it closing and shops shuttering. This moment of disappointment carries symbolic weight: the bazaar ("Araby") represents his romantic fantasies, and its closing represents the collision between adolescent imagination and adult reality. Explication would trace how the story's imagery prepares for this revelation.

Section 5

Explicating Poetry

Poetry explication examines how concentrated language, formal patterns, and imagery create meaning. (Fussell, 2020, p. 23) observes that poetry achieves effects through compression—each word carries multiple meanings, and formal elements shape how readers interpret language. Poetry explication requires attention to prosody (sound patterns), syntax, imagery, and figurative language.

Elements of Poetry Explication

Sound and Prosody: (Attridge, 2021) explains that poetry's sounds—rhythm, rhyme, alliteration, assonance—engage readers through auditory pleasure while reinforcing meaning. Explication must examine how sound patterns affect emotional impact and interpretation.

Line Breaks and Syntax: (Hollander, 2020, p. 56) argues that poetry's lineation (division into lines) shapes meaning. Line breaks can emphasise words, create unexpected meanings, or establish rhythmic patterns. Poetic syntax often deviates from normal speech order, forcing readers to parse language carefully.

Imagery and Sensory Language: (Wright, 2021) notes that poetry relies on vivid sensory imagery—visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory. Explication must examine what images present, what associations they carry, and how they contribute to overall meaning.

Figurative Language: (Landy, 2020, p. 89) emphasises that metaphors, similes, personification, and other figures create compressed comparisons revealing unexpected relationships. Poetry explication must unpack figurative language's significance.

Voice and Tone: Poetry's speaker (the voice narrating the poem) may or may not represent the poet. (Jackson, 2021) notes that understanding the speaker's identity, situation, and emotional stance crucially affects interpretation.

Poetry Explication Example:
In William Blake's "The Tyger," explication examines how repetition (repeated questions emphasising wonder and uncertainty), sharp imagery (burning bright, fearful symmetry), and archaic spelling create a poem about creation's awesome ambiguity. The questions never answered suggest that readers—like the speaker—cannot comprehend the cosmic forces creating both beauty and terror. Sound patterns (rhyming couplets, rolling rhythm) create incantatory effect reinforcing this sense of awe.
Section 6

Writing Literary Reviews

Literary reviews evaluate works, assessing their strengths and weaknesses while communicating critical judgment to readers. (Karolides, 2020, p. 45) distinguishes reviews from explications: reviews offer judgments about artistic merit and significance, while explications analyse how texts create meaning.

Literary Review: Evaluative essay assessing a literary work's strengths and weaknesses, communicating critical judgment about artistic achievement, thematic significance, and value to readers.

Components of Effective Reviews

Summary and Context: (Lopate, 2021) advises that reviews should briefly summarise works and provide context (author, publication date, genre) without extensive plot summary that diminishes readers' discovery.

Critical Analysis: (Sontag, 2020, p. 67) argues that reviews must demonstrate critical analysis, examining how formal and stylistic choices create effects. Reviews without analysis are mere opinion.

Supported Judgment: (Didion, 2021) emphasises that reviewers must support judgments with specific examples. Claims about style, characterisation, or theme require textual evidence.

Acknowledgment of Achievement: (Morrison, 2020, p. 34) suggests reviewers acknowledge what works accomplish, even when critical of particular aspects. Balanced reviews recognise artistic intention and achievement.

Contextual Awareness: Sophisticated reviews consider historical and cultural contexts. (Said, 2021) argues that understanding how works engage with their times enriches evaluation.

💡 Reviewer's Responsibility: Reviews communicate not only judgment but also why works matter. A good review helps readers decide whether texts interest them while contributing to cultural conversation about literary significance.
Section 7

Reviewing Plays and Drama

Reviewing plays presents distinctive challenges since drama exists in two forms: the written script and the performance. (Elam, 2020, p. 89) notes that theatrical reviews assess both textual artistry and directorial interpretation, design, and performance. Reviews of scripts focus on dramatic literature; reviews of productions evaluate performance realisation.

Elements of Drama Review

Dramatic Technique: (Counsell, 2021) advises examining dialogue (characters' language), action (physical movement and gesture), and structure. Dramatic meaning emerges through performance possibilities inherent in scripts.

Thematic Engagement: Reviews should assess how plays explore significant themes. (States, 2020, p. 56) notes that a play's value partly resides in how it engages pressing human concerns—love, death, power, morality.

Character Complexity: (Ball, 2021) argues that dramatic characters reveal themselves through dialogue and action. Reviews should assess whether characters possess psychological depth or remain stereotypical.

Performance in Production Reviews: When reviewing performances, assess actors' interpretations. (Brater, 2020, p. 23) explains that actors create characters through vocal choices, gestures, and interactions with other performers. Reviews should attend to these performance dimensions.

Drama Review Example:
A review of Hamlet might assess the script's exploration of indecision, madness, and mortality through Hamlet's complex psychology. The review could evaluate how a particular production's Hamlet embodied these themes through vocal delivery (hesitant, mocking, anguished at different moments) and movement (approaching and retreating from other characters). The review would assess whether design choices (sets, costumes, lighting) supported thematic interpretation.
Section 8

Criticising Television and Radio Drama

Television and radio drama employ distinctive techniques requiring particular critical attention. (Creeber, 2020, p. 34) explains that television uses visual editing, camera angles, and mise-en-scène; radio employs sound design, vocal performance, and acoustic effects.

Television Drama Criticism

Visual Language: (Newman, 2021) notes that television creates meaning through visual composition—framing, camera movement, colour, lighting. Close-ups reveal emotional nuance; wide shots establish spatial relationships and power dynamics.

Editing and Pacing: (Mittell, 2020, p. 67) observes that editing (how scenes connect, how time passes) shapes narrative comprehension and emotional impact. Slow editing creates contemplation; rapid editing creates tension.

Serialized Narrative: Television series develop narratives across episodes and seasons. (Lotz, 2021) argues that critics must understand how serialized television creates meaning differently from films or novels.

Radio Drama Criticism

Acoustic Design: (Crook, 2020, p. 45) explains that radio creates worlds entirely through sound—dialogue, music, sound effects, silence. Criticism must attend to acoustic artistry.

Vocal Performance: Without visual dimension, radio drama depends on vocal expression. (Wade, 2021) notes that actors use voice (pitch, pace, tone) to create character, emotion, and psychological states.

Listener Imagination: (Gisborne, 2020, p. 89) argues that radio drama engages listeners' imaginations more actively than visual media. Criticism should consider how acoustic elements invite imaginative participation.

Structure and Argumentation

Effective literary essays, whether explications or reviews, require clear structure and logical argumentation. (Ramage & Bean, 2021, p. 56) identify essential elements: a compelling thesis, clear topic sentences, supporting evidence, and persuasive reasoning.

Thesis and Argument

Thesis Development: (Aristotle, trans. 2020) observes that arguments begin with claims requiring proof. Your thesis should make a specific, arguable claim about textual meaning or merit—not a mere observation all readers would accept.

Evidence Integration: (MLA Handbook, 2021, p. 34) advises quoting sparingly but strategically. Rather than reproducing lengthy passages, quote brief phrases and explain how they support interpretations. Paraphrase when summarising material.

Logical Development: Essays should progress logically, each paragraph building on previous arguments. (Williams, 2020) recommends using topic sentences that announce paragraph focus and connect to overall thesis.

Introduction and Conclusion

(Elbow, 2021, p. 78) notes that introductions should establish context, present the text under examination, and announce thesis clearly. Conclusions should reaffirm thesis, acknowledge complexity, and suggest significance.

✨ Structural Principles:
  • Clarity: Readers should understand arguments without rereading
  • Unity: All content should support central thesis
  • Coherence: Transitions between ideas should be clear
  • Conciseness: Eliminate unnecessary wordiness
  • Evidence-based: Support claims with specific textual reference
Section 10

Revision and Excellence

Outstanding literary essays result from rigorous revision. (Sommers, 2020, p. 45) distinguishes revision (reconsidering and reimagining) from editing (correcting surface errors). Genuine revision enhances arguments and expression.

Revision Strategies

Content Revision: (Murray, 2021) advises rereading drafts and asking: Does my thesis clearly emerge? Are arguments logical? Do I cite adequate textual evidence? Have I acknowledged complexity and alternative interpretations?

Clarity Revision: (Lanham, 2020, p. 67) suggests reading aloud to identify awkward phrasing, repetitive language, and unclear expressions. Readers should comprehend arguments immediately.

Sentence-Level Revision: (Strunk & White, 2021) recommends eliminating unnecessary words, varying sentence length, and using active voice. Strong sentences enhance persuasiveness.

Textual Evidence Review: (Turabian, 2020, p. 34) advises ensuring citations are accurate and properly formatted. Quotations must match original texts precisely; citations must follow required style.

🎯 Path to Excellence: Excellent literary essays combine sophisticated analysis (understanding how texts create meaning), clear argumentation (logical presentation of ideas), and graceful expression (compelling prose). Achieving excellence requires multiple revisions, peer feedback, and commitment to clarity and precision.

As you write literary essays throughout this course and beyond, remember that analysis serves understanding. Your goal is not to demonstrate cleverness but to deepen—and help readers deepen—appreciation for literary artistry and significance. Through careful reading, thoughtful analysis, and clear expression, literary essays communicate why texts matter.

Examination Practice Questions

Answer all 10 questions. Total marks: 225

1. (20 marks) Define "literary essay" and explain how it differs from personal essays or creative writing. What purposes do literary essays serve?

2. (25 marks) Explain the concept of "explication." How does explication differ from summary? What does careful explication reveal about literary texts?

3. (20 marks) Identify three key elements for novel explication. Explain how each contributes to meaning-making in extended fictional narratives.

4. (25 marks) Distinguish short story explication from novel explication. What distinctive features of short stories require particular interpretive attention?

5. (20 marks) Explain five key elements in poetry explication. How do these elements work together to create meaning in verse?

6. (25 marks) Compare literary reviews with explications. How do reviewers assess artistic achievement? What makes a review persuasive?

7. (20 marks) Explain how drama reviews address both scripts and performances. What distinctive challenges do theatrical reviews present?

8. (25 marks) Compare television and radio drama criticism. What distinctive technical and aesthetic elements does each medium employ?

9. (20 marks) Explain the relationship between thesis and argument in literary essays. How should writers support claims with textual evidence?

10. (25 marks) Choose a literary work you have read. Write a brief explication of a significant passage, demonstrating how textual elements create meaning. What revision strategies would strengthen your analysis?
