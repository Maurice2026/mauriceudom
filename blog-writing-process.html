My Writing Process: How I Go from Idea to Published Book

By Maurice Udom  |  11 min read

People ask me: "How do you write a book?" They expect a secret. The honest answer is there is no trick — only a process, and the willingness to show up for it day after day, even when the words don't come easily. Over seven published works spanning tragedy, comedy, satire, and academic scholarship, I have developed a process that works.

The Spark — Where Ideas Come From

Every book begins with a question, an image, or an anger. The Blind Gods began with anger at systemic injustice against women. The Golden Home began with a character — a graduate reduced to gatekeeper, whose ambition turned toward schemes. I keep a notebook. When something strikes me — a phrase, situation, character trait — I write it down. Most entries lead nowhere. But occasionally, one refuses to leave me alone. That is the one I pursue.

Research — Understanding Before Writing

Before I write a single line of dialogue, I research. For plays, this means understanding the social, cultural, and historical context. For academic books, it means reading extensively to understand what has been said and what still needs to be said. Research is not just facts — it is empathy. To write convincing characters, you must understand how they think, what they fear, what they believe they deserve.

Outlining — Building the Architecture

I outline before writing. For a play, I map the dramatic arc — inciting incident, rising action, climax, resolution. I know the ending before I write the beginning. Characters often surprise me and the story shifts, but the outline ensures I always know where I am going. For academic works, I outline down to the paragraph level — what will each paragraph prove, and what evidence will it use?

The First Draft — Getting It Down

The first draft's only job is to exist. I write quickly, without stopping to perfect sentences or second-guess word choices. If I get stuck, I skip and come back later. The goal is momentum — moving from blank page to completed manuscript as fast as possible. Most aspiring writers give up after three chapters. The secret is to keep writing anyway. Every published book was once a terrible first draft.

Revision — Where the Real Writing Happens

I revise every manuscript at least three times. The first focuses on structure — does the argument hold? Does the plot work? The second focuses on language — clarity, word choice, rhythm, dialogue. The third focuses on details — grammar, punctuation, consistency. Revision is not punishment — it is craftsmanship. I have cut entire chapters because they did not serve the story. It hurts. But the book is always better for it.

External Feedback — The Trusted Reader

After my own revisions, I give the manuscript to two or three trusted readers whose literary judgment I respect and who will tell me the truth. Their feedback reveals blind spots I cannot see myself. For academic works, this stage involves peer review from colleagues in the field.

Professional Editing and Publication

The final stage is professional editing — a fresh, expert pair of eyes. Then formatting, cover design, and deciding how and where to publish. Publication is not the end — it is the beginning of a new conversation between the book and its readers.

What I wish I had known: Writing is rewriting. Discipline matters more than inspiration — I write on a schedule, not when I "feel like it." Every book teaches you something the previous one did not. The hardest part is not writing — it is the willingness to be honest on the page.
