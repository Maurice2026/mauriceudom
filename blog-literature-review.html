How to Structure a Literature Review: From Reading to Writing

A practical walkthrough for postgraduate students — from searching for sources to writing a review that demonstrates critical thinking.

The literature review is where most dissertation students get stuck. Not because they cannot find sources — but because they do not know how to organise dozens of articles, books, and papers into a coherent narrative that demonstrates their understanding of the field.

A literature review is not a summary of everything you have read. It is an argument about the current state of knowledge on your topic — what is known, what is debated, what is missing, and where your research fits in. This guide walks you through the entire process in five clear steps.

01
Define Your Scope

Before you read a single article, define what your literature review will cover and what it will not. Your scope should be narrow enough to be manageable but broad enough to provide context for your research question.

02
Search Systematically

Use academic databases — Google Scholar, JSTOR, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science — to find relevant sources. Keep a structured record of each source with author, year, title, key findings, and your notes on relevance.

03
Organise by Themes, Not by Source

This is the most important step. A literature review should be organised thematically — by ideas and debates that emerge across multiple sources — not source by source.

04
Write with Critical Analysis

A literature review is not a neutral report. You are expected to evaluate sources — to comment on their strengths, weaknesses, and limitations. Show that you can assess the quality and relevance of evidence.

05
Identify the Gap

The literature review should build toward a clear conclusion: here is what we know, here is what we do not know, and here is where my research fits in.

Common Mistakes

1. Summarising instead of synthesising. Listing what each author said is not a literature review. Group sources by theme.
2. Including irrelevant sources. Every source should directly relate to your research question.
3. Failing to show the gap. If your review does not justify your research, it has not done its job.
4. Relying only on recent sources. Include foundational studies that established key concepts.
5. Not being critical enough. Presenting all studies as equally valid suggests you lack evaluative ability.
