How to Write Your First Play: A Practical Guide

By Maurice Udom  |  10 min read

Writing a play is different from writing a novel or a short story. It is not just about telling a story — it is about telling it through action, dialogue, and presence. A play lives on stage, not on the page. And that fundamental difference changes everything about how you construct narrative, develop character, and build tension.

If you have never written a play before, you might feel intimidated. But plays are often shorter than novels and more focused than short stories. A play forces you to cut away everything that is not essential. Every line must earn its place. Every scene must move the action forward. There is no room for filler, and that discipline makes you a better writer.

Understand Dramatic Structure

A play has a clear architecture. It begins with exposition — establishing who the characters are and what they want. It builds through rising action — obstacles, complications, conflicts. It reaches a climax — the moment of maximum intensity and revelation. And it concludes with resolution — the consequences of everything that came before.

You do not need to follow this structure slavishly. But you need to understand it so you know when and how to break it effectively.

Create Characters Through Conflict

In a play, character is revealed through action and dialogue, not through description. You cannot tell the audience who your character is — you must show them. A character's true nature emerges in moments of pressure, choice, and consequence.

Your protagonist should want something — power, love, justice, redemption. The antagonist should want something different. And the conflict between these desires is what creates the play.

Master Dialogue

Stage dialogue is not how people actually talk. It is how people talk when every word matters. Dramatic dialogue is compressed, purposeful, and rhythmic. People rarely finish sentences in real life, but stage characters often do — because the audience needs to hear every word.

Listen to how people speak — their cadences, their habits, their specific ways of expressing ideas. Use that as your foundation. Then refine it, tighten it, make it theatrical.

Keep It Physical

A play happens on stage. Your characters move, gesture, interact with objects and with each other. Stage business — the physical actions that accompany dialogue — must be clear and purposeful. What does your character do while speaking? How do they use the space? These details matter.

Show, Don't Tell

This is the golden rule of playwriting. Do not have characters explain what is happening or what they are feeling. Let the audience see it. Let them experience it. The most powerful moments in theatre are often silent.
