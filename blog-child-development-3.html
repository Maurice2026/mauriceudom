Motor Development in Early Childhood: Building Strength and Coordination

By Maurice Udom | 10 min read

Motor development is one of the most visible transformations in early childhood. From a newborn who can barely lift their head to a five-year-old running, climbing, and writing, the journey is remarkable. Yet many parents and educators don't fully understand the sequence of motor development or how to support it effectively.

This guide explores gross and fine motor development, the typical milestones children reach, why some children develop faster or slower than others, and practical strategies to encourage healthy physical development.

Understanding Motor Development

Motor development refers to the development of a child's ability to use their muscles effectively. It happens in two main categories:

Gross Motor Skills

These involve the large muscles in the arms, legs, and core. Examples include rolling over, sitting, crawling, walking, running, jumping, and climbing. Gross motor skills develop in a predictable sequence, though the timeline varies by child.

Fine Motor Skills

These involve the small muscles in the hands and fingers. Examples include grasping, picking up objects, pointing, drawing, writing, and using utensils. Fine motor skills develop more slowly than gross motor skills and require significant practice.

Key principle: Motor development follows a predictable pattern—from head to toe, from large muscles to small muscles, and from simple to complex movements. Understanding this helps you know what's normal for each age.

Gross Motor Milestones (Birth to 5 Years)

0-3 Months

  • Lifts head briefly when on tummy
  • Makes jerky arm and leg movements
  • Kicks legs

3-6 Months

  • Holds head steady
  • Rolls from back to side
  • Bears weight on legs when held
  • Rocks back and forth

6-9 Months

  • Sits without support
  • Crawls or scoots
  • Pulls to standing with support

9-12 Months

  • Stands holding furniture
  • Cruises along furniture
  • May take first steps

12-18 Months

  • Walks independently
  • Climbs stairs with help
  • Throws a ball
  • Kicks a ball

18-24 Months

  • Runs (with falls)
  • Climbs furniture
  • Jumps in place (or attempts to)
  • Walks backward

2-3 Years

  • Runs more smoothly
  • Climbs stairs with alternating feet
  • Pedals a tricycle
  • Stands on one foot briefly
  • Kicks a ball forward

3-4 Years

  • Hops on one foot
  • Catches a ball
  • Balances on one foot for several seconds
  • Climbs skillfully
  • Rides a tricycle

4-5 Years

  • Skips
  • Rides a bicycle with training wheels
  • Throws and catches with accuracy
  • Balances on one foot for longer periods
  • Shows coordinated movements in play

Fine Motor Milestones

Fine motor development is slower and more variable than gross motor development. Children develop fine motor skills at different rates, and variation is completely normal.

0-3 Months

  • Grasps objects placed in hand
  • Brings hands to mouth

3-6 Months

  • Rakes (grasps with whole hand)
  • Transfers objects from hand to hand
  • Holds and shakes rattles

6-9 Months

  • Pincer grasp begins (thumb and fingers)
  • Picks up objects with thumb and finger
  • Bangs objects together

9-12 Months

  • Refined pincer grasp
  • Points with index finger
  • Places objects in containers
  • Begins to scribble

12-18 Months

  • Scribbles spontaneously
  • Stacks 2-3 blocks
  • Uses spoon and fork (with spilling)
  • Turns pages of a book

18-24 Months

  • Stacks 4-6 blocks
  • Uses spoon with less spilling
  • Scribbles in circular motions
  • Plays with puzzles with large pieces

2-3 Years

  • Stacks 6-8 blocks
  • Copies vertical and horizontal lines
  • Begins to draw circles
  • Uses fork and spoon with improving control
  • Turns pages one at a time

3-4 Years

  • Stacks 9-10 blocks
  • Copies circles and crosses
  • Uses scissors with supervision
  • Draws pictures (though recognizable)
  • Writes some letters

4-5 Years

  • Draws pictures with recognizable details
  • Copies letters and numbers
  • Uses scissors independently
  • Writes some letters and numbers
  • Has better pencil control

Factors That Influence Motor Development

Genetics and Temperament

Children are born with different genetic predispositions. Some children are naturally more athletic, while others develop strength at a slower pace. This is normal variation.

Opportunity and Practice

Motor skills require practice. Children who have more opportunities to move—playing outdoors, climbing, running—develop motor skills faster.

Safety vs. Risk

Paradoxically, some risk-taking is necessary for motor development. Children need opportunities to climb, jump, and explore within safe boundaries.

Nutrition and Sleep

Adequate nutrition and sleep support physical development. Growth happens during sleep, and proper nutrition provides the building blocks for healthy development.

How to Support Motor Development

Provide Space to Move

Children need room to move freely. Clear spaces for crawling, walking, running, and climbing. Outdoor play is particularly important.

Offer Varied Activities

Different activities develop different skills. Running develops cardiovascular strength. Climbing develops coordination. Drawing develops fine motor skills. Variety is key.

Model Movement

Children learn by watching. Play sports, dance, or exercise around your children. Show them what movement looks like.

Avoid Over-Reliance on Screen Time

Too much screen time limits the movement and play that develops motor skills. Limit screens and prioritize active play.

Be Patient with Individual Variation

Some children walk at 9 months, others at 15 months. Both are normal. Avoid comparing your child to others.

Celebrate Effort, Not Just Achievement

Praise the effort ("You worked hard to climb that!") rather than just the outcome. This builds resilience and love of movement.

Red Flags and When to Seek Help

While variation in motor development is normal, certain signs warrant professional evaluation:

  • Not rolling by 6 months
  • Not sitting by 9 months
  • Not walking by 18 months
  • Significant delay in any major gross motor milestone
  • Unable to pick up small objects with thumb and finger by 12 months
  • Unable to follow simple commands or imitate movements
  • Extreme muscle tension or limpness
  • Regression (loss of previously acquired skills)
  • Extreme clumsiness or difficulty with coordination

If you have concerns, consult your pediatrician. Early intervention for genuine delays can make a significant difference.

The Joy of Movement

Motor development isn't just about reaching milestones. It's about a child discovering what their body can do, building confidence, and learning to interact with the world. The toddler who takes their first steps, the three-year-old who masters riding a tricycle, the four-year-old who learns to skip—these are moments of pride and joy.

Your role isn't to force development but to provide opportunities, encouragement, and safe spaces to explore. Watch your child move, celebrate their progress, and enjoy this remarkable period of growth.
