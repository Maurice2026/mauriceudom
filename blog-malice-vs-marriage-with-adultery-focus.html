Malice VS Marriage: The Verdict

When Marriage Vices Trigger Malice

Relationships · Marriage Advice · 20 min read

Marriage fails not from a single cause, but from many. Infidelity, deception, abuse, neglect, addiction, broken promises—any of these can shatter a marriage. But when a spouse responds to these vices with malice, they add another failure on top of the first. This examination addresses all of them. If your marriage carries any of these wounds, this is written for you.

================================================================================

PART 1: THE MARRIAGE COVENANT

Marriage is a binding agreement. When two people marry, they make promises:

  • Faithfulness and exclusivity
  • Honesty and truth-telling
  • Care and presence
  • Respect and dignity
  • Commitment and engagement

These promises form the foundation. Remove any one of them, and the structure weakens. Remove multiple, and the marriage collapses.

Understand This: Marriage is built on trust. Once trust is broken—by any vice—the person who broke it has violated the covenant. The marriage, as originally promised, is damaged or destroyed.
================================================================================

PART 2: THE VICES — What Breaks Marriage

Now let us name them. These are the specific ways one spouse can violate the marriage covenant:

1. Adultery / Infidelity

The deliberate choice to share intimacy with someone outside the marriage. This directly violates the promise of exclusivity.

2. Deception / Habitual Lying

Systematic dishonesty about finances, relationships, whereabouts, or important life decisions. This destroys the foundation of trust.

3. Emotional Infidelity

Developing an intimate emotional connection with someone outside the marriage. This violates the promise of emotional exclusivity.

4. Abuse (Verbal, Emotional, Physical)

Systematic harm through words, control, or violence. This violates the promise to care for and respect your spouse.

5. Neglect / Abandonment

The refusal to be present—emotionally, physically, or financially. This violates the promise of commitment and care.

6. Financial Betrayal

Hiding debt, stealing from shared accounts, refusing to provide, or making major financial decisions without consent. This violates the promise of partnership.

7. Addiction (Alcohol, Drugs, Gambling)

The prioritization of substances over spouse and family. This violates the promise to care for the marriage.

8. Broken Promises / Unfaithfulness

Repeated failure to honor commitments—about children, career, fidelity, or shared goals. This erodes trust incrementally.

9. Disrespect / Humiliation

Constant criticism, mocking, belittling, or public embarrassment of your spouse. This violates the promise of dignity.

10. Refusal to Engage / Total Shutdown

The complete withdrawal of communication and presence. This violates the promise of partnership and engagement.

"Marriage is an I-Thou relationship—one in which we see and honor the full humanity of another person."
— Martin Buber, philosopher

Any of these vices represents a failure to see your spouse as a full human being. Each one breaks a specific promise. Each one damages the marriage.

Understanding Adultery: The Vice That Triggers Malice

While all vices break marriage, adultery holds a unique position. It is not just a betrayal of commitment—it is the most intimate, most visible, most undeniable breach of the marriage covenant. This is why adultery so powerfully triggers malice.

Why Adultery Is the Ultimate Breach:

  • It is deliberate and calculated: Unlike a moment of anger or a single dishonest word, adultery requires planning, deception, and repeated choices to hide the truth. The betrayed spouse must confront not just the act, but the systematic dishonesty that made it possible. The adulterer did not stumble into infidelity—they chose it, planned it, and concealed it.
  • It involves a specific person: When your spouse commits adultery, they have selected another person. This is not abstract harm—it is concrete and personal. The betrayed spouse must live with the knowledge that their spouse found someone else attractive enough, desirable enough, worth enough to risk the entire marriage. That comparison becomes unbearable.
  • It violates the deepest promise: Marriage's most foundational promise is exclusivity in intimacy. The betrayed spouse gave their body, their vulnerability, their trust to one person and one person only. When that promise is broken, it is not just a betrayal—it is a violation of the most sacred agreement between two people.
  • It cannot be undone: The act is complete. The betrayed spouse cannot unknow what their spouse did. They cannot erase the memory. They cannot restore the marriage to what it was before. Adultery creates a permanent scar that time alone will not heal.
  • It forces public knowledge: While some betrayals can remain hidden, adultery typically becomes known. When it does, the betrayed spouse must face not only the betrayal but also the public humiliation of having been deceived by the person they trusted most.
  • It introduces existential doubt: Adultery does not just hurt—it makes the betrayed spouse question everything. "Was anything real? Were the tender moments lies? Was I deceived the entire time?" Trust, once shattered by infidelity, becomes almost impossible to restore.
The Psychological Chain: Adultery → Complete destruction of trust → Unbearable pain and humiliation → Desperate need to regain control → Malice as the only weapon that feels powerful

This is the progression that leads to malice.

How Adultery Triggers Malice

When a spouse discovers adultery, they experience something most people cannot fully comprehend until it happens to them: the complete shattering of reality. Everything they believed about their marriage, their spouse, their own worth—all of it collapses.

The betrayed spouse asks the questions that have no good answers:

  • "How long has this been happening?"
  • "Did they think of me while with them?"
  • "What did I lack that made them seek someone else?"
  • "Were they ever truly committed to me?"
  • "Will they do this again?"
  • "How can I ever trust again?"

In this state of devastation, the betrayed spouse faces a choice: engage or withdraw. Most, faced with adultery, choose to withdraw. And their withdrawal often becomes malice.

Why? Because in a marriage destroyed by adultery, the betrayed spouse has lost all other sources of power. They cannot control whether their spouse still thinks of the other person. They cannot control whether contact continues. They cannot control their spouse's heart. But they can control one thing: their own presence, their words, their affection.

Malice becomes the betrayed spouse's desperate attempt to regain power through the only means available: withdrawal.

The betrayed spouse thinks: "If I cannot make you love me, I can make you feel my pain. If I cannot restore what you destroyed, I can punish you for destroying it. If I cannot make you understand through words, I can make you understand through silence."

"After infidelity, couples face a choice: Do we rebuild together, or do we apart? What they cannot do is remain together in silence. Prolonged silence after infidelity is not recovery—it is slow death."
— Sue Johnson, marriage counselor

The Tragic Truth About Malice After Adultery

Here is what the betrayed spouse does not understand when they choose malice: Malice does not heal adultery. Malice does not answer the questions adultery raised. Malice does not restore trust. Malice only ensures that the marriage, already broken by infidelity, is now broken again by the response to it.

The spouse who committed adultery destroyed the marriage through action. The spouse who responds with malice destroys it through inaction—through refusal to speak, to engage, to decide, to move forward.

Adultery + Malice = Death of the Marriage

Not death from the infidelity alone, but death from the decision to respond to infidelity with prolonged punishment, silence, and withdrawal.

The betrayed spouse often believes: "My silence is justified. They deserve to suffer. They deserve to feel what I feel." And this is sometimes true. But there is a difference between consequence and torture, between appropriate anger and weaponized withdrawal.

A consequence has an endpoint. "I am hurt and angry. We need counseling. If you cannot be honest with me, I will leave." This is consequence.

Torture has no endpoint. Three months of silence. Six months of coldness. Years of withdrawal. No explanation of what it would take to restore the relationship. No timeline for healing. Just ongoing punishment.

This is malice, and it is as destructive as the adultery that preceded it.

================================================================================

PART 3: THE RESPONSE — When Vices Trigger Malice

When a spouse experiences any of these vices, they are hurt. They are betrayed. Their trust is shattered. They have every right to their pain.

But now they face a critical choice: How will they respond?

The Options:

  • Immediate Separation: "I cannot remain in this home. I need time to decide if this marriage can continue."
  • Confrontation and Counseling: "What you have done has broken us. We need professional help."
  • Direct Conversation: "I am devastated. I need you to understand what you have done and what comes next."
  • Clear Departure: "I cannot rebuild this. We need to separate."

But many betrayed spouses choose a different path: malice.

Malice is the withdrawal of affection, communication, and presence as punishment. It manifests as:

  • Three months of silence
  • Years of coldness
  • Refusal to speak about the issue
  • Remaining in the home but refusing engagement
  • Using withdrawal as a weapon
Critical Point: When a spouse chooses malice, they have added a second failure to the marriage. The first failure was the vice of the other spouse. The second failure is the choice to weaponize pain rather than address it.
"Stonewalling—the silent treatment—is one of the most destructive patterns in relationships. Couples who resort to it are three times more likely to separate than those who engage in direct conflict."
— John Gottman (2,000-couple study)
================================================================================

PART 4: THE PROGRESSION — Cause and Effect

Now trace this carefully:

STAGE 1: Two people marry with mutual promises.

STAGE 2: One spouse commits a vice (any of the ten listed above). The covenant is broken. The trust is shattered. The marriage is damaged.

STAGE 3: The betrayed spouse responds with malice—silence, coldness, refusal to engage. Now the marriage is broken from both sides. First through vice. Second through refusal to address the vice.

STAGE 4: Both parties are now guilty. Both have failed. The marriage ends.

The Hard Truth: When adultery happens, malice does not save the marriage. When deception is discovered, silence does not restore trust. When abuse occurs, withdrawal does not heal. Malice adds damage on top of damage.

The betrayed spouse might argue: "But they deserve punishment." This is sometimes true. But there is a difference between consequence and malice:

  • Consequence: "What you did has caused me pain. I need time. We will talk when I am ready."
  • Malice: "I will not tell you when we will talk. I will make you live in uncertainty. I will starve you of communication indefinitely."

One is accountability. One is torture.

================================================================================

PART 5: THE VERDICT — Both Have Failed

The Spouse Who Committed the Vice: FAILED

This spouse broke the covenant. Whether through adultery, deception, abuse, neglect, or any other vice—they violated their promise. They chose their desire, their addiction, their anger, or their selfishness over their commitment.

They cannot claim innocence. They initiated the destruction.

The Spouse Who Chose Malice: ALSO FAILED

This spouse had every right to their pain. But they did not have the right to weaponize it indefinitely while remaining in the marriage.

When adultery happens, the betrayed spouse has every right to their pain. But they do not have the right to weaponize that pain against a person they refuse to leave.

They face a choice:

  • Stay and work: Insist on counseling, honesty, and rebuilding
  • Stay and engage: Have difficult conversations and make informed decisions
  • Leave and heal: Separate with dignity and move forward

What they cannot do: Stay and punish. Remain and withdraw. Trap both people in prolonged suffering.

"A soft answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger." What does prolonged silence stir up? Desperation. Resentment. The slow death of what was promised.
— Proverb

Stubbornness exists on both sides. The spouse who committed the vice is stubborn in refusing to acknowledge harm. The spouse who chose malice is stubborn in refusing to make a choice.

================================================================================

PART 6: WHAT SHOULD HAVE BEEN DONE

For the Spouse Who Committed the Vice:

  • Confess immediately
  • Take full responsibility without excuses
  • Answer all questions with honesty
  • Agree to immediate counseling
  • Demonstrate change through consistent action

For the Betrayed Spouse:

  • Allow yourself to feel your pain fully
  • Make a clear decision: Can we rebuild, or must we separate?
  • If rebuilding: insist on counseling and demonstrable change
  • If separating: end the marriage clearly and with dignity
  • Do NOT choose malice
================================================================================

FINAL VERDICT

Any vice breaks a marriage. Adultery, deception, abuse, neglect, addiction—any of these can destroy what was promised.

But malice also destroys a marriage. And it destroys with equal force.

The person who commits a vice has made a choice that cannot be unmade. They have broken the covenant.

But the person who chooses malice has also made a choice. They have chosen control over honesty. They have chosen punishment over partnership. They have refused to engage.

Both are failures. Both deserve better. And the marriage deserves better.

The Fundamental Truth: You cannot maintain malice and maintain a marriage. You cannot commit vice and maintain a marriage. If you choose either—committing vice or responding with malice—the marriage is over. The only question is whether you will end it honestly or destroy it slowly.

If your marriage carries any of these vices, understand this: The vice is not your fault if you did not commit it. But your response to it is your responsibility.

Choose honesty. Choose engagement. Choose to either rebuild with integrity or leave with dignity.

But do not choose malice.

That is the path of slow death.
