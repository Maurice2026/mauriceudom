1. Introduction

Effective communication is never automatic. Between the moment a sender forms a clear idea and the moment a receiver accurately understands it, there exists a landscape of potential obstacles — forces that distort, delay, dilute, or entirely block the passage of meaning from one mind to another. These obstacles are called communication barriers, and they are the principal reason why so much organisational communication fails to achieve its intended purpose.

Chapter 1 introduced the concept of noise as any factor that disrupts the communication process. In this chapter, we examine that concept in its full depth and breadth, moving beyond the simple notion of interference to a systematic analysis of the six major categories of communication barrier that research has consistently identified: semantic, psychological, physical, organisational, cultural, and technological. Each category operates differently, affects communication in distinctive ways, and requires its own set of remedies. Understanding the full taxonomy of communication barriers is an essential diagnostic skill — because a barrier that is misidentified will be treated with the wrong remedy, and the communication failure will continue.

Mbarushimana and Allida (2022), in their comprehensive review of research on communication barriers and organisational effectiveness, found that most significant communication failures in organisations involve not one barrier operating in isolation, but several barriers from different categories operating simultaneously and reinforcing each other. This is why a single intervention — sending the message again, or using a different channel — so rarely resolves a persistent communication problem. Effective remediation requires identifying and addressing all the barriers that are operating, not merely the most visible one.

2. A Framework for Understanding Communication Barriers

Key Concept — Communication Barriers Defined and Classified

A communication barrier is any factor — internal or external, intentional or unintentional, structural or individual — that prevents a message from being transmitted, received, or understood as the sender intended. Barriers operate at every stage of the communication process: they can interfere with encoding (preventing the sender from formulating the message accurately), with transmission (preventing the message from reaching its intended receiver), or with decoding (preventing the receiver from interpreting the message as intended).

The six major categories of communication barrier, examined in this chapter, are:

1. Semantic barriers — arising from the words, language, and symbols used to encode the message.

2. Psychological barriers — arising from the mental and emotional states of the communicators.

3. Physical barriers — arising from the environment, geography, and physical conditions in which communication takes place.

4. Organisational barriers — arising from the structure, culture, and systems of the organisation itself.

5. Cultural barriers — arising from differences in cultural background, values, norms, and communication styles.

6. Technological barriers — arising from the limitations, failures, and misuse of communication technologies.

Key Concept — Why Correct Diagnosis Matters

The analogy of medical diagnosis is instructive. A doctor who treats a bacterial infection with rest and fluids — the correct treatment for a viral infection — will not cure the patient, because the treatment is mismatched to the cause. Similarly, a manager who responds to a communication failure caused by psychological barriers (fear of speaking up) by redesigning the formal communication structure will not resolve the problem, because the structural intervention does not address the psychological cause.

Correct diagnosis requires asking not just "what went wrong?" but "why did it go wrong?" and "at what stage of the communication process did it go wrong?" The answers to these questions identify the barrier category, and the barrier category determines the appropriate remedy. Neuberger, Lemos, and Watkins (2023) emphasised that effective organisational communicators develop the diagnostic habit of examining communication failures through multiple barrier lenses simultaneously — rather than reaching for the first explanation that presents itself and acting on it without checking whether other barriers are also operating.

3. Semantic Barriers

Semantic barriers are among the most common and most underestimated sources of communication failure. They arise from the language and symbols used to encode a message — from the words themselves, the way they are arranged, and the meanings they carry for different audiences.

Key Concept — Semantic Barriers Defined

Semantic barriers are obstacles to communication that arise when sender and receiver assign different meanings to the same words, symbols, or expressions. The word "semantic" relates to meaning — so semantic barriers are, in the most literal sense, barriers of meaning: situations in which the sender encodes a message that they believe is clear and unambiguous, but which carries a different meaning — or multiple possible meanings — for the receiver.

Semantic barriers are particularly insidious because they are often invisible. The sender and receiver may both believe that communication has taken place — that a message has been sent and understood — when in fact they have each understood something different. The failure only becomes apparent when the receiver acts on their understanding and the sender discovers that the action does not correspond to their intention.

Key Concept — Five Types of Semantic Barrier

1. Jargon and technical language: Every professional field, industry, and organisation develops its own specialised vocabulary — acronyms, technical terms, and insider phrases that enable efficient communication among those who share the same background, but that are opaque or misleading to those who do not. A medical professional who describes a patient's condition using clinical terminology to a non-medical colleague, or an IT specialist who explains a system failure using technical jargon to a non-technical manager, is creating a semantic barrier that may entirely prevent the receiver from understanding the message. Oduaran and Okojie (2023) found that in Nigerian public sector organisations, the use of bureaucratic and legalistic language in official communications is a persistent semantic barrier that prevents many policies and procedures from being accurately understood and implemented by the frontline employees to whom they are addressed.

2. Ambiguity and multiple meanings: Many words and phrases carry multiple possible meanings, and the receiver will select the meaning that makes most sense to them in context — which may be very different from the meaning the sender intended. "We need to review your performance" can mean anything from a routine appraisal conversation to the beginning of a disciplinary process; the receiver's interpretation will be shaped by their anxiety, their past experiences, and their relationship with the sender rather than by the sender's actual intention. Ambiguous language is particularly dangerous in written communication, where the receiver cannot ask for immediate clarification and where the sender cannot observe the receiver's reaction and adjust the message accordingly.

3. Denotation and connotation: Words carry both denotative meanings — their literal, dictionary definition — and connotative meanings — the associations, emotions, and implications they carry for particular audiences in particular contexts. The word "restructuring" denotes an organisational change process; it connotes, for many employees, job losses and insecurity. A manager who uses the word "restructuring" in a memo about an efficiency improvement project, intending only its denotative meaning, may unintentionally communicate a threat that sends waves of anxiety through the workforce before any other word has been read. Awareness of the connotative as well as the denotative weight of language is an essential component of effective semantic encoding.

4. Abstractness and vagueness: Abstract, generalised language — "we need to improve our service," "performance has been unsatisfactory," "communication must be better" — fails to communicate anything specific or actionable. Every receiver will interpret it through their own frame of reference and will form a different understanding of what is required. Concrete, specific language — "we need to reduce customer waiting times from an average of twelve minutes to six minutes by the end of the quarter" — eliminates the semantic ambiguity of abstraction and gives all receivers the same clear, testable understanding of what is expected.

5. Language and literacy differences: In multilingual organisations and in societies with wide educational diversity, literal language differences — including varying levels of literacy, vocabulary, and language proficiency — create semantic barriers that require active management. In Nigeria's linguistically diverse organisational environment, where English is the official language of formal communication but many employees operate primarily in Hausa, Yoruba, Igbo, or other languages, language differences can create significant semantic barriers in formal written communication that is technically accessible but practically impenetrable to significant portions of the workforce.

Workplace Example — Semantic Barrier in a Nigerian Organisation

A state government ministry issues a circular to all local government councils requiring them to "expedite the submission of their quarterly financial returns in compliance with the new harmonised reporting framework." The circular is written in precise bureaucratic English and is technically complete — it identifies the requirement, references the relevant framework, and implies a deadline (expeditiously).

In practice, three different councils interpret the circular in three different ways. The first council, whose finance officer is fluent in bureaucratic language and familiar with the new framework, understands and complies within a week. The second council's finance officer understands the requirement but does not know what "the new harmonised reporting framework" refers to — she has not received the documentation introducing it — and so cannot comply. The third council's clerk, for whom English is a second language and bureaucratic vocabulary is largely unfamiliar, understands the circular as a general reminder to submit reports at some point and does not treat it as urgent.

Three semantic barriers are operating simultaneously: jargon (the "harmonised reporting framework" is referenced but not explained); ambiguity (the deadline is implied, not stated); and language difference (the clerk's limited familiarity with formal bureaucratic English prevents accurate decoding). The ministry receives two late submissions and one non-submission, and interprets all three as non-compliance — not recognising that two of the three are semantic failures, not wilful failures of compliance.

4. Psychological Barriers

Psychological barriers arise not from the language of the message but from the minds of the communicators — the internal mental and emotional states that shape how messages are encoded by senders and decoded by receivers. They are among the most powerful and most difficult barriers to overcome, precisely because they operate largely below the surface of conscious awareness.

Key Concept — Psychological Barriers Defined and Categorised

Psychological barriers are obstacles to communication that arise from the attitudes, emotions, perceptions, and cognitive processes of the communicators. They include:

1. Perception and selective attention: Human beings do not process all available information equally — we attend selectively to stimuli that are consistent with our existing beliefs, expectations, and concerns, and we tend to filter out or minimise information that challenges or contradicts them. This selective attention means that two people receiving the same message may process it very differently — each attending to the aspects of the message that are most relevant to their own concerns and filtering out aspects that do not align with their existing worldview. A manager who believes that a particular employee is underperforming may read a positive performance report about that employee as an exception or an anomaly, rather than updating their assessment of the employee's capabilities.

2. Emotional state: Strong emotions — anxiety, anger, grief, excitement, or love — profoundly affect both the encoding and the decoding of messages. An anxious employee may hear criticism in a message that was intended as neutral feedback; an angry manager may communicate in ways that are more aggressive and less clear than she would under calmer conditions; a grieving colleague may be entirely unable to process complex information regardless of how carefully it is presented. Yue, Men, and Ferguson (2021) found that the emotional climate of an organisation — the prevailing tone of its communication culture — significantly affects the quality of individual communication acts: organisations with fearful, competitive, or cynical emotional climates consistently show worse communication outcomes than those with psychologically safe, supportive communication cultures.

3. Stereotyping and prejudice: Pre-existing assumptions about the characteristics, capabilities, or intentions of particular groups — based on gender, age, ethnicity, educational background, professional role, or organisational level — shape how messages from members of those groups are received. A manager who harbours implicit assumptions about the capabilities of young women, or of employees without university education, may unconsciously discount or distort messages from those groups — hearing them less carefully, attributing less credibility to their content, and being less likely to act on them than messages from individuals whose identity matches the manager's implicit model of a credible communicator.

4. Defensiveness and closed-mindedness: Individuals who feel threatened, criticised, or challenged tend to respond defensively — shutting down their receptiveness to the incoming message and focusing their cognitive energy on self-protection rather than genuine engagement. A manager who receives critical feedback from a subordinate may become defensive and reframe the feedback as disrespect or incompetence rather than genuinely engaging with its content. Defensiveness is a particularly damaging psychological barrier in organisations because it is most likely to arise precisely in those communication situations — performance feedback, problem escalation, challenge to authority — where genuine engagement is most important.

5. Poor listening habits: Most people are significantly less skilled at listening than at speaking — and significantly more aware of their speaking than their listening. Poor listening — which includes interrupting, formulating responses while the other person is still speaking, distraction, and selective attention — is one of the most pervasive psychological barriers in organisational communication. Ruck and Welch (2022) found that employees consistently rate "being listened to" as one of the most important dimensions of manager effectiveness, and "not being listened to" as one of the most damaging failures of management communication.

Workplace Example — Psychological Barriers Compounding

A factory safety officer has identified a significant risk in the loading bay — a procedural gap that he believes could lead to a serious accident. He raises this concern in the weekly operations meeting, but the operations manager — who is under significant pressure to meet a production target this month and who has a somewhat dismissive attitude towards the safety officer (whom he privately considers overly cautious) — listens only partially, frames the concern as "safety officer raising another procedural issue," and moves on to the next agenda item without committing to any action.

Three psychological barriers are operating simultaneously: the operations manager's selective attention (filtering the safety concern through his belief that the safety officer is overly cautious); his emotional state (anxiety about the production target making him less receptive to anything that might slow output); and his stereotyping (framing the safety officer's role as inherently obstructive rather than as a genuine operational intelligence function). The safety officer, having experienced this response before, does not escalate further — a fourth psychological barrier: his learned helplessness about the futility of upward communication on safety matters in this organisation. Two weeks later, the accident occurs.

5. Physical Barriers

Key Concept — Physical Barriers Defined and Categorised

Physical barriers are obstacles to communication that arise from the physical environment in which communication takes place — from the geographical, environmental, and material conditions that prevent messages from being transmitted or received clearly.

1. Geographical distance: People who are physically distant from each other face barriers to the richest and most effective forms of communication — face-to-face interaction. Remote and hybrid working arrangements have dramatically increased the prevalence of geographical distance as a communication barrier, making organisations increasingly dependent on technology-mediated communication that, as Chapter 2 demonstrated through Media Richness Theory, is inherently less rich than face-to-face interaction. Neuberger, Lemos, and Watkins (2023) found that fully remote teams consistently struggle more than co-located teams with the relationship-building, trust development, and emotional attunement that underpin effective communication — not because remote workers are less skilled communicators, but because the channels available to them are structurally leaner than those available to co-located workers.

2. Environmental noise: Physical noise in the environment — loud machinery, construction sounds, background conversations, poor room acoustics, or traffic noise — makes it physically difficult to hear, concentrate, and process spoken messages. Environmental noise is a particularly significant barrier in manufacturing, construction, healthcare, and hospitality environments, where important operational communications must compete with high ambient noise levels. The consequences can be severe: in safety-critical environments, missed or misheard instructions caused by environmental noise contribute directly to accidents and injuries.

3. Poor infrastructure: Inadequate communication infrastructure — unreliable electricity supply, slow or intermittent internet connections, poor mobile network coverage, outdated telephone systems, inadequate meeting facilities — creates physical barriers to both face-to-face and technology-mediated communication. In many Nigerian organisational contexts, infrastructure limitations are a significant and persistent physical barrier: power outages interrupt video calls, weak network signals prevent voice calls from connecting, and poorly equipped meeting rooms undermine the quality of face-to-face communication. Oduaran and Okojie (2023) noted that infrastructure deficits are one of the most consistently cited barriers to effective communication in Nigerian public sector organisations.

4. Time zone and scheduling differences: For organisations operating across multiple time zones, the physical reality of different day-night cycles creates barriers to synchronous communication. When teams in Lagos, London, and Los Angeles need to communicate in real time, the overlap in working hours may be minimal — creating physical barriers to the timely, interactive communication that many coordination tasks require.

5. Physical layout and workspace design: The design of the physical workspace — whether it encourages or discourages informal interaction, whether it creates privacy for sensitive conversations or exposes them to unwanted audiences, whether it brings different departments into proximity or separates them — profoundly affects the frequency, quality, and nature of organisational communication. An open-plan office facilitates informal horizontal communication but makes private conversations difficult; a series of enclosed individual offices protects privacy but reduces spontaneous interaction. Physical layout is a communication infrastructure choice, and organisations that design it deliberately — with communication quality as a criterion — consistently outperform those that treat it as a purely logistical decision.

6. Organisational Barriers

Organisational barriers are embedded in the structure, systems, and culture of the organisation itself — they are not the product of any individual's failure but of the way the organisation has been designed, managed, and allowed to develop over time. This makes them among the most persistent and most difficult barriers to address, because addressing them requires changing not individual behaviour but organisational systems.

Key Concept — Organisational Barriers Defined and Categorised

Organisational barriers are obstacles to communication that arise from the formal and informal structures, processes, cultures, and systems of the organisation. They include:

1. Tall hierarchies and information filtering: As established in Chapter 4, messages that pass through multiple hierarchical levels are subject to progressive filtering, summarisation, and distortion. The taller the hierarchy, the greater the cumulative distortion of both downward and upward communications. Organisations with ten hierarchical levels face a structural communication challenge that flat organisations with three levels do not — not because their people are less skilled communicators, but because every additional hierarchical level adds an additional opportunity for distortion.

2. Information overload: The explosion of digital communication tools has created an unprecedented volume of communication in modern organisations. Welch (2022) found that information overload — the experience of receiving more information than one can meaningfully process — is one of the most consistently reported communication problems across all types of organisation. When employees are overwhelmed with emails, messages, reports, announcements, and updates, they develop coping mechanisms — selective reading, automatic deletion, skimming rather than engaging — that cause important communications to be missed or inadequately processed. Information overload does not make organisations better informed; it makes them worse informed, because the important signal is lost in the noise of the trivial.

3. Specialisation and departmental silos: As discussed in Chapter 5, the division of organisations into specialised departments creates structural barriers to horizontal information flow. Siloed departments that do not communicate effectively with each other produce coordination failures, duplicated effort, conflicting priorities, and fragmented customer experiences. The silo barrier is both structural (separate reporting lines, separate systems, separate physical spaces) and cultural (norms of departmental loyalty and competition that inhibit information sharing).

4. Formalisation and bureaucratic rigidity: Highly formalised organisations — in which all significant communications must follow prescribed formats, pass through specified channels, and obtain specified approvals — can be so rigid that they prevent timely, accurate, and relevant communication from reaching those who need it. Bureaucratic communication processes that were designed to ensure accuracy and accountability can become barriers when they prioritise procedure over effectiveness and when the pace of procedural compliance cannot keep pace with the speed at which operational realities change.

5. Absence of feedback mechanisms: Organisations that communicate without building in feedback mechanisms are communicating blindly. As emphasised in Chapter 4, the absence of mechanisms through which receivers can signal whether they have received, understood, and acted on a message means that senders cannot know whether their communications have achieved their purpose — and cannot correct misunderstandings before they cause operational problems.

6. Poor meeting culture: Meetings are the primary forum for face-to-face organisational communication, and poor meeting culture — characterised by unclear purposes, inadequate preparation, poor facilitation, and absent action follow-up — is a significant organisational barrier to communication effectiveness. Mroz, Allen, Verhoeven, and Shuffler (2023) found that poorly managed meetings not only fail to achieve their communication purposes but actively damage communication culture, by conveying messages of disrespect, disorganisation, and disregard for participants' time and contributions.

7. Cultural Barriers

Cultural barriers arise when the assumptions, values, norms, and communication styles of one cultural group differ significantly from those of another — creating misunderstandings, misinterpretations, and communication failures that neither party may fully recognise because each is operating within their own cultural framework and may not realise that the other party's framework is different.

Key Concept — Cultural Barriers Defined and Categorised

Cultural barriers to communication arise from differences between the cultural frameworks — the values, beliefs, norms, communication styles, and social practices — of sender and receiver. They operate both between organisations from different national or ethnic cultures and within organisations whose members come from diverse cultural backgrounds.

1. High-context versus low-context communication cultures: Edward Hall's distinction between high-context and low-context cultures is one of the most practically important frameworks for understanding cultural communication barriers. In high-context cultures (common in many African, Asian, Middle Eastern, and Latin American societies), much of the meaning of a communication is carried by contextual cues — the relationship between the communicators, their shared history, non-verbal signals, and what is left unsaid — rather than by the explicit content of the words. In low-context cultures (common in many Northern European and North American societies), meaning is carried primarily by explicit verbal content, and good communication is characterised by directness and completeness. When people from high-context and low-context cultures communicate, profound misunderstandings can arise: the high-context communicator may feel that the low-context communicator is unnecessarily blunt or disrespectful of their shared relationship; the low-context communicator may feel that the high-context communicator is evasive, unclear, or insufficiently direct. Men and Yue (2024) observed that managing cultural communication differences is an increasingly important competence for leaders of diverse organisations.

2. Power distance norms: As discussed in Chapter 4, high power-distance cultural norms create barriers to upward communication and honest feedback across hierarchical levels. In high power-distance contexts, cultural norms around deference and respect for authority can prevent junior employees from raising concerns, offering contrary opinions, or reporting bad news to senior colleagues — regardless of the formal structures and policies an organisation puts in place to encourage such communication.

3. Non-verbal communication differences: The meaning of non-verbal signals — eye contact, physical proximity, silence, gestures, touch, and facial expression — varies significantly across cultures. In some cultures, sustained direct eye contact signals honesty and engagement; in others, it signals disrespect or challenge. In some cultures, silence after a statement signals thoughtful consideration; in others, it signals disagreement or discomfort. In some cultures, a firm handshake and direct posture signal confidence; in others, they signal aggression. These non-verbal mismatches create barriers in face-to-face cross-cultural communication that can be deeply confusing because neither party may be aware that the non-verbal signals are being interpreted through a different cultural framework.

4. Ethnocentrism and cultural assumptions: Ethnocentrism — the tendency to evaluate other cultures through the lens of one's own culture, and to regard one's own cultural communication norms as natural, correct, and universal — is one of the most pervasive sources of cultural communication barriers. A manager who assumes that the directness, informality, and egalitarianism that characterise their own cultural communication style are universal norms — and who interprets the different communication behaviours of employees from other cultural backgrounds as deviant, ineffective, or disrespectful — will create cultural communication barriers that damage relationships, suppress valuable contributions, and undermine the organisation's cross-cultural effectiveness.

5. Organisational culture clashes: In addition to national and ethnic cultural differences, different organisational cultures — the distinct communication norms, values, and styles that each organisation develops over time — can create barriers when employees from different organisational backgrounds work together. In mergers, acquisitions, and joint ventures, cultural clashes between the communicating organisations are among the most consistently documented barriers to post-integration effectiveness. Mbarushimana and Allida (2022) identified cultural incompatibility as a primary cause of communication failure in merged or acquired Nigerian organisations.

Workplace Example — Cultural Barriers in a Multinational Context

A European multinational company acquires a mid-sized Nigerian manufacturing firm. The European head office sends a new Managing Director — a German national — to lead the integration. He comes from a low-context, low power-distance communication culture in which direct feedback, open debate across hierarchical levels, and explicit verbal communication are valued norms.

In his first month, he holds a series of town hall meetings at which he invites all employees to raise concerns, challenge decisions, and offer ideas freely. The Nigerian employees attend — but say very little. He interprets their silence as agreement and satisfaction, and reports to head office that the integration is proceeding smoothly. In fact, the Nigerian employees have significant concerns about several aspects of the integration, but the cultural norms of their high-context, high power-distance environment make it deeply uncomfortable to raise critical perspectives in a public forum in the presence of the most senior person in the room — a foreigner they have only just met, who has authority over their employment.

Their concerns travel instead through the informal communication network, where they become progressively more anxious and exaggerated. By the third month, a significant proportion of the most experienced employees — those with the best alternative employment options — have quietly begun looking for other positions. The first two of the senior technical staff resign in month four. Only then does the Managing Director learn — from an exit interview — that the integration has not been proceeding smoothly at all, and that the silence he interpreted as satisfaction was in fact a culturally conditioned absence of upward communication in a high power-distance context.

8. Technological Barriers and Strategies for Overcoming All Barriers

Key Concept — Technological Barriers Defined and Categorised

Technological barriers arise from the limitations, failures, and misuse of communication technologies. As organisations become increasingly dependent on digital communication tools, technological barriers have grown from a minor category to a major and increasingly consequential source of communication failure.

1. Technical failures and unreliability: System crashes, network outages, software glitches, hardware failures, and cybersecurity incidents can completely disrupt communication at critical moments. Organisations that are entirely dependent on digital communication — with no fallback mechanisms for when systems fail — are highly vulnerable to technological barriers that can sever communication precisely when it is most urgently needed. The COVID-19 pandemic revealed the extent of this vulnerability when millions of organisations simultaneously discovered that their digital communication infrastructure was less robust than they had assumed.

2. Digital literacy gaps: Not all employees are equally comfortable or competent with digital communication tools. Older employees, employees from less technologically developed backgrounds, and employees in roles that have historically required little digital interaction may struggle with platforms that tech-savvy colleagues find intuitive. These digital literacy gaps create unequal access to organisational communication — effectively excluding some employees from information flows that their work depends on.

3. Channel fragmentation and communication platform overload: Modern organisations use a bewildering array of digital communication tools — email, WhatsApp, Teams, Slack, Zoom, Skype, intranets, SMS, and others — often with no clear norms about which tool to use for which type of communication. Channel fragmentation means that important messages may be sent on one platform while the intended receiver is monitoring another; that the same message is distributed redundantly across multiple channels, contributing to information overload; and that employees spend significant cognitive energy simply managing their communication channels rather than engaging with the communications they contain.

4. Loss of non-verbal richness: As established in Chapter 2, text-based digital communication strips out the non-verbal signals — facial expression, vocal tone, body language, physical proximity — that carry a significant proportion of meaning in face-to-face interaction. This loss of richness makes digital communication structurally more prone to misinterpretation, particularly for emotionally charged or relationally sensitive messages. The consequences range from minor misunderstandings (an email read as curt that was intended as concise) to serious conflicts (a disciplinary message communicated by text that is experienced as contemptuous).

5. Surveillance anxiety and communication inhibition: In organisations where employees are aware that their digital communications are monitored — or where they fear they might be — this awareness can inhibit honest communication, particularly on sensitive topics. The permanent digital record created by email, messaging platforms, and video calls can make employees reluctant to express uncertainty, raise concerns, or offer critical perspectives in writing, for fear that their words will be taken out of context or used against them. Neuberger, Lemos, and Watkins (2023) identified surveillance anxiety as an increasingly significant technological barrier to authentic organisational communication.

Key Concept — Strategies for Overcoming Each Category of Barrier

Overcoming semantic barriers: Use plain, specific, concrete language; avoid jargon in communications addressed to mixed or non-specialist audiences; define technical terms when they must be used; check for understanding by asking receivers to paraphrase key messages in their own words; be alert to the connotative as well as the denotative weight of language choices; provide context and background that enables receivers to interpret the message as intended.

Overcoming psychological barriers: Develop active listening skills and practise genuine receptivity to incoming messages; create emotionally safe communication environments; acknowledge and address emotional content in communication; actively challenge personal biases and stereotypes; seek out disconfirming information and diverse perspectives; build a communication culture characterised by psychological safety and genuine openness to honest exchange. Ruck and Welch (2022) emphasise that psychological safety — the organisational condition in which people feel safe to communicate honestly without fear of retribution — is the foundation on which all other communication improvement rests.

Overcoming physical barriers: Invest in communication infrastructure — reliable power supply, high-speed internet, well-equipped meeting facilities; design workspaces deliberately to facilitate the types of communication the organisation most needs; establish clear protocols for communication across time zones; use appropriately rich channels to compensate for physical distance; schedule regular in-person interactions to supplement remote communication.

Overcoming organisational barriers: Flatten hierarchies where possible to reduce filtering; establish and enforce information management policies that combat overload; break down silos through cross-functional teams, liaison roles, and shared incentives; build feedback mechanisms into all significant communication processes; redesign meeting cultures to emphasise clarity of purpose, quality of facilitation, and accountability for action.

Overcoming cultural barriers: Invest in cross-cultural communication training for all staff in diverse organisations; develop explicit communication norms that acknowledge and accommodate different cultural styles; build cultural intelligence as a leadership competency; consult cultural insiders before designing communication approaches for new cultural contexts; interpret silence and indirect communication through cultural lenses rather than through the lens of one's own cultural assumptions. Men and Yue (2024) found that organisations that explicitly address cultural communication differences report significantly better communication outcomes than those that apply a single cultural communication standard across all their operations.

Overcoming technological barriers: Establish clear channel norms that specify which digital tool to use for which type of communication; invest in digital literacy training for all employees; maintain backup communication channels for when primary systems fail; apply Media Richness Theory in tool selection — use rich channels for complex, sensitive messages and lean channels only for simple, routine ones; protect employee privacy and trust by using communication data responsibly and transparently; regularly audit the digital communication tool portfolio and rationalise it to reduce fragmentation and overload.

Workplace Example — Multiple Barriers and Multi-Level Remediation

A Nigerian logistics company is experiencing serious problems with its order fulfilment process — customers are receiving incorrect or delayed deliveries at an unacceptable rate. An internal investigation identifies that the root cause is a communication breakdown between the Sales department (which takes orders), the Warehouse department (which picks and packs orders), and the Delivery department (which dispatches them). A communication consultant is brought in to diagnose the barriers.

The consultant identifies barriers from five of the six categories operating simultaneously. A semantic barrier: Sales uses customer reference numbers while Warehouse uses internal product codes — the same order is described in different language by different departments, creating constant confusion about which products correspond to which order. A psychological barrier: the Warehouse supervisor has a history of conflictual relationships with the Sales team, and this hostility causes him to interpret Sales communications with a defensive suspicion that leads him to double-check rather than trust order information — creating delays and occasional substitutions. A physical barrier: the Sales office and the Warehouse are in separate buildings 400 metres apart, with unreliable Wi-Fi between them, meaning that real-time digital communication between them is frequently disrupted. An organisational barrier: there is no formal communication protocol between departments — communication about orders happens informally, through WhatsApp messages and phone calls, with no documentation trail and no escalation process for discrepancies. A technological barrier: the company's three departments use three different order management systems that do not automatically synchronise, requiring manual data entry at each handover point — creating both delays and transcription errors.

The remediation programme addresses each barrier specifically: a unified order reference system (semantic); a structured mediation process and cross-departmental team-building for the supervisor-Sales relationship (psychological); a Wi-Fi extender and a dedicated communication workstation in the Warehouse (physical); a formal communication protocol with documented handover records (organisational); and a project to integrate the three order management systems (technological). Within six months, the fulfilment error rate falls by 78%. No single intervention would have achieved this result — only addressing all five barriers simultaneously produced the transformation.

Explain the roles of selective attention, emotional state, stereotyping, defensiveness, and poor listening in creating psychological barriers. Using the factory safety officer example from this chapter or an example of your own, demonstrate how psychological barriers can compound each other with severe consequences.
  4. (15 marks) Explain physical barriers to communication. How does inadequate infrastructure create physical communication barriers in Nigerian organisational contexts? Drawing on the work of Oduaran and Okojie (2023) and Neuberger, Lemos, and Watkins (2023), discuss how remote working has intensified physical barriers for many organisations.
  5. (20 marks) Identify and explain six organisational barriers to communication. For each barrier, explain how it arises from organisational structure, culture, or systems, and suggest one targeted intervention that a manager could implement to reduce its impact. Draw on the work of Welch (2022) and Mroz, Allen, Verhoeven, and Shuffler (2023) in your answer.
  6. (15 marks) Explain cultural barriers to communication. Distinguish between high-context and low-context communication cultures and explain how their interaction creates cultural barriers. Drawing on the work of Men and Yue (2024) and Mbarushimana and Allida (2022), discuss the specific cultural communication challenges facing Nigerian organisations in international partnerships and joint ventures.
  7. (15 marks) Explain five technological barriers to communication and discuss how the explosion of digital communication tools has both reduced and created new communication barriers. What specific organisational policies and practices can help organisations manage technological barriers effectively?
  8. (20 marks) Using the Nigerian logistics company case study from Section 8, or an organisation you are familiar with, demonstrate how communication barriers from multiple categories can operate simultaneously and reinforce each other. Explain why a single-barrier intervention would be insufficient and design a multi-level remediation programme that addresses each barrier category specifically.
  9. (15 marks) Critically evaluate the statement: "Most communication barriers in Nigerian organisations are cultural rather than structural, and the solution is better cross-cultural training rather than better systems design." Do you agree or disagree? Justify your position with reference to at least three barrier categories discussed in this chapter.
  10. (10 marks) A new department head joins an organisation and quickly notices that her team members rarely speak up in meetings, that important information often fails to reach her until it is too late, and that the team seems to communicate more through the grapevine than through official channels. Using the barrier framework from this chapter, diagnose the most likely barrier categories operating in this situation and recommend three specific, targeted interventions she should implement in her first ninety days.
