1. Introduction

When the psychologist Albert Mehrabian published his research on face-to-face communication in the 1960s, he produced a finding that has remained one of the most cited — and most misunderstood — in the entire field of communication studies: that in the communication of feelings and attitudes, only 7 per cent of the emotional meaning is conveyed by the words themselves, while 38 per cent is carried by tone of voice and 55 per cent by body language and facial expression. While subsequent researchers have nuanced and contextualised these percentages — pointing out that they apply specifically to the communication of emotion and cannot be generalised to all types of communication — the core insight remains well-supported: in face-to-face interaction, a very substantial proportion of what is communicated passes not through the words that are spoken but through the non-verbal signals that accompany, modify, and sometimes contradict them.

For organisational communicators, this insight has profound and practical implications. A manager who praises an employee's work while maintaining a flat tone, avoiding eye contact, and leaning back with crossed arms is communicating something very different from what the words say. A job applicant who speaks confidently and competently but fidgets, avoids the interviewer's gaze, and takes up as little space as possible is communicating a self-image that may undermine an otherwise strong verbal performance. A negotiator who understands how to read the non-verbal signals of the party across the table has access to information that the words alone cannot provide.

This chapter examines the major categories of non-verbal communication — kinesics, facial expression and eye contact, proxemics, paralanguage, appearance, chronemics, and environment — and their significance in organisational settings. It also addresses the particular challenges that cultural diversity and digital communication environments pose for non-verbal communication management. Read alongside Chapter 7 on verbal communication, this chapter provides a complete picture of the communication repertoire that effective organisational communicators must develop and manage.

2. Defining Non-Verbal Communication and Its Relationship to Verbal Communication

Key Concept — Non-Verbal Communication Defined

Non-verbal communication refers to all the ways in which meaning is transmitted between communicators through means other than words — through the body, the voice's qualities, the use of space and time, physical appearance, and the environmental context of the interaction. Non-verbal communication is continuous: unlike verbal communication, which can be switched on and off (a person can choose when to speak and when to remain silent), non-verbal signals are transmitted whenever two people are in each other's presence, whether or not either party is consciously trying to communicate anything.

This continuity of non-verbal communication has an important implication for organisational settings: it is impossible not to communicate non-verbally. A manager who says nothing about a proposal is still communicating non-verbally — through posture, expression, and demeanour — and those non-verbal signals will be read and interpreted by others regardless of the manager's intention.

Neuberger, Lemos, and Watkins (2023) noted that the rapid shift towards digital and text-based communication in modern organisations has created environments in which non-verbal communication is often severely attenuated or entirely absent — with significant consequences for the quality of communication, the depth of relationships, and the psychological wellbeing of employees who no longer have access to the full richness of in-person interaction.

Key Concept — Five Relationships Between Verbal and Non-Verbal Communication

Non-verbal signals do not operate independently of verbal communication — they interact with it in five important ways:

1. Repeating: Non-verbal signals can repeat and reinforce what the words say — nodding while saying "yes," pointing in the direction being described. Repetition strengthens the message by providing redundant confirmation.

2. Complementing: Non-verbal signals can add emotional or attitudinal content to verbal messages — a warm tone of voice and a genuine smile complement verbal praise and amplify its motivational effect.

3. Substituting: Non-verbal signals can replace verbal messages entirely — a thumbs-up, a shrug, a wave of acknowledgement, or a dismissive eye-roll can communicate a complete message without a word being spoken.

4. Regulating: Non-verbal signals regulate the flow of conversation — eye contact, head nods, and postural shifts signal when speakers are ready to yield the floor, when they wish to interrupt, when they are waiting for more information, and when they have finished speaking.

5. Contradicting: Non-verbal signals can contradict the verbal message — a manager who says "I am perfectly happy with your performance" while leaning away, avoiding eye contact, and speaking in a flat, tense tone is sending a contradictory non-verbal message. When verbal and non-verbal signals conflict, research consistently shows that receivers give more weight to the non-verbal — because non-verbal signals are harder to consciously control and are therefore perceived as more authentic indicators of the speaker's true feelings and intentions.

3. Kinesics — Body Language, Gesture, and Posture

Kinesics — the study of body movement as communication — encompasses all the ways in which the movement, position, and orientation of the body communicate meaning in social interaction. It is the most visible and most extensively studied category of non-verbal communication.

Key Concept — Kinesics in Organisational Settings

Gestures are deliberate or habitual movements of the hands, arms, or head that communicate meaning. They fall into several categories. Emblems are gestures with a specific, culturally shared verbal equivalent — the thumbs-up, the OK sign, the pointing finger. Illustrators accompany and illustrate speech — the sweeping hand movement that suggests a large scale, the pinching gesture that suggests smallness. Regulators manage the flow of conversation — the raised hand that signals a desire to speak, the nodding head that signals continued listening. Adaptors are self-touching behaviours — adjusting clothing, scratching, playing with a pen — that typically signal discomfort, anxiety, or boredom and are usually performed without conscious awareness. In organisational settings, adaptors observed in a job interview, a negotiation, or a performance conversation may signal that the speaker is more stressed or uncertain than their words suggest.

Posture — the overall orientation and alignment of the body — communicates attitudes towards the interaction and towards the other participants. Open posture (body turned towards the other person, arms uncrossed, weight slightly forward) signals engagement, interest, and openness. Closed posture (arms crossed, body turned away, weight pulled back) signals defensiveness, disengagement, or reluctance — regardless of what is being said verbally. Postural mirroring — the unconscious tendency to adopt a posture similar to that of the person one is interacting with — signals rapport and alignment; it is commonly observed in conversations between people who like and trust each other, and its absence is a reliable indicator of distance or tension in a relationship.

Orientation and physical alignment — the direction in which the body is turned during an interaction — communicates inclusion and exclusion. A group that orients their bodies towards each other, forming a closed circle, is communicating exclusivity; a group that maintains a more open formation, with bodies turned slightly outward, is communicating openness to newcomers. In meetings, the physical orientation of participants relative to each other and to the person chairing the meeting communicates the hierarchy and relationship structure of the group in ways that supplement and sometimes contradict the formal agenda.

Workplace Example — Reading Kinesics in a Management Context

A department head is presenting a new performance management framework to her team of eight managers. She is watching their non-verbal responses carefully as she speaks. Three managers are leaning forward, maintaining eye contact with her, and nodding occasionally — clear kinesic signals of engagement and receptivity. Two managers are sitting back with their arms crossed, exchanging brief glances with each other — kinesic signals of scepticism or resistance. One manager is looking at his phone under the table, his body angled away from the presenter — a clear signal of disengagement. Two managers are maintaining a relatively neutral posture but are particularly still — a signal that they are listening carefully but have not yet formed a view.

Without these kinesic signals, the department head would have to deliver her presentation to a uniform surface of apparent attention and wait until the formal question session to discover that significant resistance and disengagement exist in the room. With them, she can address the resistance directly — pausing to invite responses from the two crossed-arm managers, acknowledging the aspects of the framework that she knows are difficult, and checking in specifically with the manager whose phone use signals disengagement. The kinesic signals give her real-time intelligence about the communication dynamics of the room that enables her to adjust and respond before the formal session ends.

4. Facial Expressions and Eye Contact

The human face is capable of producing thousands of distinct expressions, making it the richest non-verbal communication channel available for conveying emotional states, attitudes, and reactions. Paul Ekman's research identified six basic emotions — happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, and surprise — whose facial expressions are recognised across cultures, suggesting a biological foundation for at least some forms of facial non-verbal communication.

Key Concept — Facial Expressions in Organisational Communication

In organisational settings, facial expressions serve several important communication functions. They provide real-time feedback to speakers about how their messages are being received — the listener's expression of confusion tells the speaker to clarify before proceeding; the listener's expression of concern tells the speaker that something they have said has raised an issue that needs addressing. They communicate emotional authenticity — a genuine smile, which involves both the mouth muscles and the muscles around the eyes (producing what researchers call a "Duchenne smile"), is reliably distinguished from a social or performative smile, and the distinction matters in organisational contexts where authenticity is valued. They regulate social and professional interactions — the slight raising of the eyebrow that signals a question, the lip-press that signals considered thought, the widened eyes that signal surprise or concern.

One of the most practically important aspects of facial expression in organisational settings is the phenomenon of micro-expressions — brief, involuntary facial expressions that last only a fraction of a second and reveal genuine emotional states that the communicator may be attempting to conceal. A manager who is expressing support for a proposal while experiencing a micro-expression of contempt may be detected — consciously or unconsciously — by a skilled observer, even though the micro-expression lasts only milliseconds. Awareness of micro-expressions is particularly relevant in high-stakes communication contexts such as negotiations, interviews, and performance conversations.

Key Concept — Eye Contact in Organisational Communication

Eye contact is one of the most powerful and most culturally variable non-verbal signals in human communication. Its organisational functions include:

Signalling attention and engagement: Appropriate eye contact communicates that the listener is attending to the speaker — that the speaker has the listener's genuine attention rather than merely their physical presence. The withdrawal of eye contact is typically read as a signal of disengagement or distraction.

Communicating credibility and confidence: In many Western and professional cultural contexts, direct eye contact is associated with honesty, confidence, and competence. Speakers who avoid eye contact are frequently perceived — often inaccurately — as evasive, uncertain, or untrustworthy. Job applicants, presenters, and leaders who maintain appropriate eye contact consistently make more favourable impressions than those who do not, controlling for other factors.

Establishing and signalling dominance: Sustained, unbroken eye contact can communicate dominance, challenge, or intensity — the hard stare of a confrontational negotiator, the sustained gaze of a leader who is making an important commitment. This dominant function of eye contact is culturally mediated: in some cultures, the appropriate response to sustained eye contact from a superior is to look down or away as a signal of deference; in others, reciprocating the gaze is expected as a signal of equal engagement.

Regulating conversation: Eye contact plays an important regulatory role in conversation — a speaker who establishes eye contact with a specific listener is signalling that they are addressing that person; a speaker who breaks eye contact and looks away is often signalling that they are finished and the floor is open; a listener who maintains eye contact while leaning slightly forward is signalling a desire to speak.

Men and Yue (2024) observed that leaders who use eye contact skillfully — maintaining it long enough to signal genuine engagement without crossing into uncomfortable intensity — build significantly stronger perceptions of transparency and trustworthiness than those who avoid or misuse it.

5. Proxemics — Space, Territory, and Physical Arrangement

Proxemics — the study of how people use and interpret physical space in social interaction — was developed by the anthropologist Edward T. Hall in the 1960s. His research revealed that the distance at which people position themselves relative to each other in different types of interaction is not arbitrary but follows culturally shared rules — rules that are violated at social cost, because territorial violations produce discomfort, anxiety, and often hostility.

Key Concept — Hall's Four Distance Zones

Hall identified four principal zones of interpersonal distance, each appropriate for particular types of social interaction:

Intimate distance (0–45 cm): The zone of physical contact, comfort, and close personal relationship. This distance is appropriate for interactions between people who are in intimate personal relationships. Uninvited encroachment into another person's intimate zone — standing too close, touching without permission — is experienced as a serious violation and provokes strong defensive responses. In organisational settings, the uninvited invasion of intimate distance is a form of harassment that carries both interpersonal and legal consequences.

Personal distance (45–120 cm): The zone of personal conversation between friends, colleagues, and people who know each other reasonably well. Most informal workplace conversation takes place within personal distance. Maintaining too large a personal distance in an informal conversation can signal coldness, aloofness, or discomfort; maintaining too small a distance can feel invasive.

Social distance (1.2–3.6 m): The zone of formal interaction — business meetings, professional conversations with relative strangers, formal discussions. Most office desk interactions, meeting table conversations, and client-facing conversations take place within social distance. The positioning of furniture in offices — the desk between manager and visitor, the conference table between participants — is a physical implementation of social distance norms.

Public distance (3.6 m and beyond): The zone of public speaking and performance — presentations, lectures, formal addresses. At public distance, speakers must compensate for physical remoteness by using amplified voice, large gestures, and high-contrast visual cues to ensure that their non-verbal signals carry across the increased space.

Key Concept — Territory, Physical Arrangement, and Organisational Power

Territoriality — the tendency to claim, defend, and personalise particular spaces — is a significant dimension of proxemics in organisational settings. The size, location, and privacy of an individual's workspace communicates their status and power in the organisational hierarchy. A corner office with a window communicates higher status than an open-plan desk in the middle of the floor; a private office communicates higher status than a shared space; a large desk behind which a manager sits communicates authority over the visitor who sits in the smaller chair on the other side.

These proxemic arrangements are not merely decorative — they actively shape the communication dynamics of every interaction that takes place within them. A manager who conducts a performance feedback conversation from behind a large desk, with the employee seated in a lower or smaller chair on the other side, has used the physical arrangement to establish a power differential that will affect everything said in that conversation. A manager who moves out from behind the desk and sits beside the employee at a small table has used the physical arrangement to equalise the relationship and signal a collaborative rather than an evaluative interaction.

Verčič and Špoljarić (2020) found that employees are acutely sensitive to the proxemic signals of their workspaces — that the physical arrangement of organisational space communicates messages about status, power, and the value the organisation places on different categories of employee that may be as powerful as any formal communication.

Workplace Example — Proxemics Shaping a Difficult Conversation

A Human Resources director at a Nigerian insurance company needs to have a difficult conversation with a senior underwriter whose performance has been deteriorating. She has two options for where to hold the conversation: her own office, with her large desk between them and the underwriter in the visitor's chair; or a small, neutral meeting room with a round table and equivalent chairs on either side.

She chooses the meeting room deliberately. The round table eliminates the proxemic signal of hierarchical difference that her desk arrangement would create; the equivalent chairs eliminate the physical status differential; the neutral location — neither her territory nor his — reduces the territorial dominance dynamic. She also positions herself at the table so that she and the underwriter are at an angle to each other rather than directly opposite — a proxemic arrangement that research has found produces less confrontational and more collaborative conversation than a face-to-face seating position.

The conversation is still difficult — the performance issues are real and must be addressed honestly — but the proxemic arrangement creates conditions in which a genuine, two-way exchange is more likely to happen. The underwriter is less defensive than he might have been in the director's office, more willing to disclose the personal circumstances that have been affecting his work, and more receptive to the support being offered. The physical arrangement of the space did not determine the outcome, but it created the conditions in which a better outcome became more possible.

6. Paralanguage — the Voice Beyond Words

Paralanguage refers to all the vocal characteristics of speech that carry meaning independently of the words being spoken — the qualities of the voice that communicate emotion, attitude, personality, and relational intention regardless of the verbal content they accompany. Paralanguage is sometimes described as "not what you say but how you say it," though this formulation undersells the complexity and sophistication of the vocal communication system.

Key Concept — The Principal Dimensions of Paralanguage

Pitch refers to the highness or lowness of the voice — determined by the frequency of vocal cord vibration. Pitch carries multiple communicative meanings. A rising pitch at the end of a sentence signals a question in most English-speaking contexts; a falling pitch signals a statement or a command. Higher overall pitch tends to be associated with excitement, anxiety, or youth; lower overall pitch tends to be associated with authority, calmness, and maturity. Leaders who lower their habitual pitch when making important announcements are using pitch consciously as a credibility signal.

Volume refers to the loudness or softness of the voice. Volume is one of the primary signals of emphasis — speaking more loudly typically signals that a point is important; speaking more softly can signal intimacy, confidentiality, or gravity. Inappropriately loud speech signals aggression or disrespect; inappropriately soft speech signals uncertainty or a lack of confidence. In professional settings, the ability to project one's voice clearly without raising it aggressively is a communication skill that significantly affects how a speaker is perceived.

Rate and pace refer to how quickly or slowly a speaker talks and how they vary that speed. A faster rate can signal urgency, excitement, nervousness, or a desire to get something over with; a slower rate signals deliberateness, seriousness, or — if too slow — boredom or condescension. Skilled speakers vary their rate deliberately, slowing down to emphasise important points and speeding up to maintain energy and momentum in less critical passages.

Tone and quality refer to the emotional colouring of the voice — whether it sounds warm or cold, sincere or sarcastic, interested or bored, sympathetic or dismissive. Tone is the primary vehicle through which the relational and emotional dimension of spoken communication is carried. The same words — "that's a very interesting idea" — can communicate genuine enthusiasm, polite tolerance, or undisguised contempt depending entirely on the tone in which they are delivered.

Pauses and silence are often overlooked as a dimension of paralanguage but are among its most powerful elements. A deliberate pause before an important statement creates anticipation and signals significance. A pause after asking a question signals that a genuine answer is expected rather than a rhetorical performance. Ruck and Welch (2022) found that managers who use silence skillfully — who pause after asking questions and wait genuinely for the answer rather than rushing to fill the silence themselves — are consistently rated by employees as better listeners and more effective communicators than those who fill every silence immediately.

Disfluencies — filled pauses such as "um," "uh," "er," and "you know" — are vocal signals of hesitation, uncertainty, or the cognitive effort of searching for the right words. Occasional disfluencies are normal in natural speech and are tolerated without significant impact on perceptions of credibility; frequent disfluencies in formal communication contexts can signal unpreparedness or lack of confidence.

7. Appearance, Chronemics, and Environment

Key Concept — Appearance as Non-Verbal Communication

Physical appearance — including dress, grooming, accessories, and overall presentation — communicates social identity, professional role, personal values, and organisational affiliation before a single word is spoken. In organisational contexts, appearance operates as a powerful initial cue that shapes the impressions, expectations, and interpretations of everyone the individual encounters.

Dress and grooming communicate role, status, and cultural affiliation. Organisational dress codes — formal or informal, explicit or implicit — encode the organisation's values and norms about professionalism, hierarchy, and identity. A bank where all staff wear formal business attire communicates different values from a technology company where jeans and T-shirts are the norm; the appearance of its employees is itself a form of organisational communication that conveys the culture, the formality, and the identity of the institution. Oduaran and Okojie (2023) noted that in many Nigerian public sector organisations, the formal dress code is itself a communication of institutional authority and seriousness that carries significant symbolic weight.

Artefacts and accessories — watches, bags, jewellery, stationery, and equipment — communicate status and identity in organisational settings. The distinction between expensive and inexpensive artefacts communicates status; the choice of cultural or religious artefacts communicates identity and affiliation. These communications are not necessarily conscious or intentional on the part of the wearer, but they are consistently read and interpreted by others.

Key Concept — Chronemics: The Communication of Time

Chronemics is the study of how people use and communicate through time. In organisational settings, time communicates power, respect, priority, and cultural values in ways that are often not consciously recognised but are deeply felt.

Punctuality is one of the most common chronemic communications in organisational life. Arriving on time for a meeting signals respect for other participants' time and commitment to the organisational schedule. Arriving late signals — regardless of the reason — that the person considers their other commitments more important than this meeting or its participants. Consistently keeping others waiting while one is always on time oneself is a classic chronemic assertion of dominance in hierarchical relationships.

Response time in digital communication has become an important chronemic signal. Responding quickly to an email or message signals that the correspondent is valued and that the communication is a priority; delayed responses signal lower priority. The expected response time varies by organisational context, relationship, and urgency — but deviations from established expectations are always communicative, whether or not they are intended to be.

Time allocation — how much time is given to a meeting, a conversation, or an activity — communicates its perceived importance. A manager who schedules a performance conversation for fifteen minutes is communicating something different from one who schedules it for an hour; an organisation that gives its senior leadership team three hours for strategic planning but only thirty minutes for employee feedback reviews is communicating its relative valuation of these activities as powerfully as any formal statement of values.

Key Concept — Environment as Non-Verbal Communication

The physical environment in which communication takes place is itself a non-verbal communication — about the organisation's values, its treatment of its members, and the importance it attaches to different activities and relationships.

A well-maintained, comfortable, and well-equipped workspace communicates that the organisation values its employees' experience. A cluttered, poorly maintained, or inadequately equipped workspace communicates the opposite — and does so with a consistency and persuasiveness that no verbal assertion of employee value can entirely counteract. Verčič and Špoljarić (2020) noted that the physical environment of the workplace is one of the strongest non-verbal signals that employees use to assess the authenticity of the organisation's stated commitment to their wellbeing and development.

The design of specific communication environments — meeting rooms, reception areas, office layouts — communicates particular messages about the type of communication intended to take place within them. A meeting room furnished with a large formal table and high-backed chairs communicates a different communication norm from one furnished with comfortable chairs arranged in a circle; an open office communicates a different communication norm from a network of enclosed individual offices.

8. Non-Verbal Communication in Cross-Cultural and Digital Contexts

Key Concept — Cultural Variation in Non-Verbal Communication

While some non-verbal signals — the basic facial expressions of emotion identified by Ekman — appear to be universal or near-universal, the majority of non-verbal communication conventions are culturally specific. The same non-verbal signal can carry entirely different meanings in different cultural contexts, creating significant risks of misinterpretation in cross-cultural communication situations that are increasingly common in globalised Nigerian organisations.

Eye contact: In many Western professional contexts, direct eye contact signals honesty, confidence, and engagement; avoiding eye contact is read as evasiveness or lack of confidence. In many African, Asian, and Middle Eastern contexts, sustained direct eye contact with a superior signals disrespect or challenge; averting the gaze is a culturally appropriate sign of deference. A Nigerian employee who averts their eyes during a conversation with a European manager may be perceived as evasive or dishonest, when they are in fact behaving with culturally appropriate respect. Men and Yue (2024) identified this misreading of culturally appropriate deference as a common source of cross-cultural communication failure in multinational organisations operating in Nigeria.

Gestures: Many gestures that are entirely benign or positive in one cultural context are offensive in another. The "thumbs up" gesture that signals approval in many Western contexts is offensive in some Middle Eastern cultures. The "OK" sign is positive in many cultures and deeply offensive in others. In Nigerian organisational contexts that involve international partnerships, the safe practice is to avoid assuming that any gesture carries a universal meaning and to invest in cross-cultural gesture awareness as part of general communication training.

Proxemics: Personal space norms vary significantly across cultures. Northern European and North American cultures tend to maintain relatively large personal distances in professional interactions; many African, Middle Eastern, and Latin American cultures are comfortable with significantly smaller personal distances. In cross-cultural professional interactions, these differences can create discomfort on both sides — the person from the smaller-distance culture may perceive the other as cold and distant; the person from the larger-distance culture may perceive the other as intrusive or aggressive — when in fact both are simply expressing different cultural norms about appropriate personal distance.

Silence: The meaning of silence varies dramatically across cultures. In some cultures, silence in a business conversation is a sign of thoughtful consideration and respect; in others, silence after a proposal is read as discomfort, disagreement, or rejection. Negotiators from high-context cultures may use silence strategically, as a communication tool; negotiators from low-context cultures may feel compelled to fill every silence with words, potentially revealing information or making concessions that undermine their negotiating position.

Touch: Norms about appropriate physical contact in professional settings vary widely. In some cultures, a firm handshake and occasional arm-touching are normal professional greetings; in others, physical contact in professional settings is minimal or limited by gender. In Nigeria's diverse cultural and religious landscape, touch norms vary significantly across communities, and awareness of these differences is an important competence for professional communicators.

Key Concept — Non-Verbal Communication in Digital Environments

Digital communication environments present a fundamental challenge for non-verbal communication: most of the rich non-verbal signal systems examined in this chapter — kinesics, facial expression, proxemics, full paralanguage, touch, and environmental context — are either severely attenuated or entirely eliminated when communication moves from face-to-face to digital channels.

Text-based communication (email, instant messaging, WhatsApp) eliminates all non-verbal signals except those that can be represented through text — punctuation, capitalisation, emoji, and the timing of responses. The result is a communication medium that is radically leaner than face-to-face communication and therefore significantly more susceptible to misinterpretation, particularly for messages with emotional, relational, or attitudinal content. The rise of emoji and digital reaction symbols represents a partial, imperfect attempt to restore some of the non-verbal richness that text communication strips away.

Video communication (Zoom, Teams, Google Meet) restores some non-verbal signals — facial expression, upper-body posture and gesture, some vocal paralanguage — while eliminating others: physical proximity and touch, full body posture and movement, the peripheral vision that enables awareness of the full social context of an interaction, and the subtle environmental cues that face-to-face meetings provide. Neuberger, Lemos, and Watkins (2023) found that video communication significantly improves on text-based communication for emotionally and relationally complex messages, but consistently falls short of face-to-face communication for the deepest relational and emotional communication tasks.

Zoom fatigue — the distinctive exhaustion that many people experience after extended video communication sessions — is at least partly a non-verbal communication problem. The effort of attending constantly to a screen-mediated, reduced-richness communication environment, of maintaining appropriate facial expression and posture for the camera, and of processing the cognitive load of partial non-verbal information that the brain is attempting to complete to a full social picture, is significantly more demanding than the equivalent effort in face-to-face communication. Awareness of this fatigue effect, and deliberate strategies for managing video communication load — shorter sessions, camera-off periods, interspersed text-based interactions — are important components of effective non-verbal management in digital work environments.

Workplace Example — Managing Non-Verbal Communication in a Hybrid Team

A senior manager at a Lagos-based financial services company leads a hybrid team — four members work from the Lagos office, three work remotely from Abuja, and two work from a partner office in London. She has been experiencing communication problems: the remote team members feel excluded from decisions that appear to be made in informal conversations among the Lagos-based staff; the Lagos-based staff feel that the remote members are disengaged; and one of the London-based members has raised a concern that his contributions in video meetings are not being acknowledged.

A communication consultant diagnoses the problem as substantially non-verbal. In the hybrid video meetings, the four Lagos-based members sit together around a laptop, giving them eye contact, physical proximity, and shared environmental cues that enable a richer non-verbal communication experience than the remote members receive — each appearing in a small rectangle on a screen, disconnected from the physical environment of the meeting. The informal conversations that produce decisions happen in the Lagos office space, where physical co-presence enables the spontaneous, low-barrier communication that is impossible at a distance. The London member's contributions are being passed over not because of any intention to exclude but because his video image is small and his speaking turn is harder to establish from across a screen.

The manager implements four changes. She restructures the physical setup of hybrid meetings so that each person — including those in the same room — appears in their own individual video window, equalising the non-verbal communication environment. She establishes a meeting norm that ensures remote participants are explicitly invited to contribute before decisions are finalised. She moves routine team communications to a shared digital platform so that informal conversations happen in a space accessible to all. And she schedules a quarterly in-person gathering — at which all nine team members meet face-to-face — specifically to build the relational foundation that makes digital communication more effective. The changes do not make digital communication as rich as face-to-face — nothing can — but they significantly reduce the non-verbal inequity that was undermining team cohesion and communication quality.

What specific non-verbal signals are eliminated or attenuated in each medium, and what are the practical consequences for communication quality, relationship-building, and team cohesion?
  10. (15 marks) Using the hybrid team case study from Section 8, or a digital communication challenge from your own experience, demonstrate how a manager can deliberately manage non-verbal communication in a hybrid or fully remote work environment. What specific, practical interventions are most effective, and why?
