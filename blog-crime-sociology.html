Broken Systems: How Society and Environment Lower the Threshold for Violence

By Maurice Udom — Nigerian Author, Scholar, Linguist & Criminologist

Series: Why People Commit Serious Crimes Peer-Reviewed Framework Approx. 3,100 words
Abstract

This article examines the sociological and environmental forces that behavioural scientists identify as lowering the threshold at which violent behaviour becomes possible. It does not argue that poverty, social deprivation, or domestic adversity cause crime in any simple, deterministic sense — the majority of individuals exposed to these conditions do not commit violent acts. It argues, rather, that these conditions systematically erode the social, psychological, and economic resources that ordinarily constrain destructive behaviour, thereby increasing the probability of violent outcomes when biological vulnerabilities and psychological pathologies are also present. Drawing on Merton's strain theory, research on the escalation of domestic coercion to lethal violence, the literature on adverse childhood experiences, and Bandura's social learning framework, the article maps the sociological dimension of the biopsychosocial model onto the specific crime types of homicide, murder-suicide, and arson. It concludes by examining the intersection of structural disadvantage with individual vulnerability and the preventive implications of that intersection.

1.0

Introduction

The sociological dimension of violent crime is perhaps the most contested terrain in criminology. On one side stand those who locate the roots of crime primarily in structural conditions — poverty, inequality, social exclusion — and who regard individual pathology as largely an artefact of those conditions. On the other stand those who resist what they regard as the sociological deflection of moral responsibility, insisting that structural circumstances explain correlation but not individual choice. The biopsychosocial framework, as established in the preceding articles in this series, does not require a resolution of this philosophical dispute. It requires only the recognition that structural and environmental forces constitute a genuine and measurable dimension of crime causation — one that interacts with biological and psychological factors rather than replacing them (Farrington, 2003).

The sociological forces examined in this article do not cause violence directly. They operate by systematically reducing the resources — economic, relational, psychological, and social — that ordinarily serve as protective buffers against destructive behaviour. Chronic poverty does not produce a murderer. It produces conditions under which the gap between aspiration and attainment becomes intolerable, under which protective relationships are strained to breaking point, and under which the institutional supports that might otherwise intervene are absent or inaccessible (Merton, 1938; Messner & Rosenfeld, 1994). In combination with the psychological vulnerabilities examined in Article 2 and the neurobiological factors to be examined in Article 4, these conditions contribute to the convergence that produces extreme violence.

This article examines four sociological pathways through which environmental conditions lower the threshold for violent behaviour: structural strain and the experience of blocked legitimate aspiration; the dynamics of domestic coercion and their escalation to lethal violence; the long-term consequences of adverse childhood experiences and the intergenerational transmission of violence; and the processes of social learning and alienation through which violent norms are acquired and internalised.

“Structural conditions do not pull the trigger. They load the weapon over years — through accumulated deprivation, frustrated aspiration, eroded dignity, and the systematic removal of alternatives.”
2.0

Strain Theory: The Criminogenic Gap Between Aspiration and Reality

Robert Merton's (1938) strain theory remains one of the most influential sociological frameworks for understanding the structural conditions that generate criminal behaviour. Merton observed that modern societies promote universal cultural goals — material success, status, financial security, social recognition — while simultaneously distributing the legitimate means of achieving those goals unequally. Individuals who internalise culturally prescribed goals of success but find themselves structurally blocked from the legitimate pathways toward them experience what Merton termed anomie — a condition of normative disjunction between aspiration and attainable reality.

The behavioural responses to this disjunction are varied. Merton identified five adaptation modes — conformity, innovation, ritualism, retreatism, and rebellion — of which innovation and rebellion are most directly associated with criminal behaviour. Innovation involves the substitution of illegitimate means for legitimate ones in the pursuit of culturally approved goals. Rebellion involves the rejection of both the goals and the means of the dominant social order, sometimes manifesting as politically motivated or expressive violence (Merton, 1938). Messner and Rosenfeld (1994) extended this framework in their institutional anomie theory, arguing that societies in which economic institutions dominate and crowd out family, educational, and civic institutions produce higher rates of serious crime by amplifying the cultural emphasis on material success while weakening the social structures that teach alternative values and provide emotional containment.

For the specific crime types examined in this series, strain theory is particularly illuminating in relation to homicides arising from acute economic crisis. The individual who faces sudden financial ruin, the collapse of a business, or devastating legal consequences — events that destroy not only material security but social standing and self-concept — experiences a form of acute structural strain that concentrates the disjunction between aspiration and reality into a single, catastrophic moment. When this acute strain acts upon a person already carrying biological and psychological vulnerabilities, it constitutes the kind of environmental activation explored further in Article 5 of this series (Agnew, 1992).

3.0

Domestic Coercion and the Escalation to Lethal Violence

A substantial proportion of homicides — and the overwhelming majority of murder-suicides — occur within the context of intimate partner relationships and domestic households. The sociological and relational dynamics that produce lethal domestic violence are among the most extensively researched phenomena in criminology and social work, and they follow a pattern of sufficient regularity that forensic investigators and clinical practitioners have developed structured risk assessment tools to identify it in advance (Campbell et al., 2003).

Johnson's (1995) influential typology of domestic violence distinguished between common couple conflict — characterised by bidirectional, situationally specific altercations — and what he termed intimate terrorism: a unidirectional pattern of coercive control in which one partner systematically deploys a range of tactics to dominate, isolate, and subordinate the other. Intimate terrorism, Johnson argued, is the form of domestic violence most closely associated with escalation to serious and lethal violence, because it is not primarily about conflict resolution or emotional dysregulation in a specific moment, but about the exercise and maintenance of comprehensive domination.

When a controlling partner perceives that their domination is threatened — most commonly through the departure or threatened departure of the controlled partner — the risk of lethal violence escalates dramatically. Stark (2007) documented the phenomenon of separation assault: the pattern in which the act of leaving an abusive relationship constitutes the moment of greatest danger for the victim, because the departing partner's action is experienced by the controlling perpetrator as a catastrophic loss of dominance, social status, and identity. The violence that follows is, from the perpetrator's perspective, the ultimate reassertion of control — the one form of dominance that cannot be escaped.

  1. Coercive control established — The perpetrator systematically isolates the victim from support networks, monitors movements, controls finances, and erodes self-esteem over time.
  2. Escalation of physical violence — Physical violence enters the relationship, typically with cycles of escalation and reconciliation that bind the victim to the relationship through alternating fear and hope.
  3. Threat of separation — The victim attempts to leave or signals intention to leave. This is statistically the period of highest lethality risk.
  4. Separation assault — The perpetrator, experiencing catastrophic loss of control, escalates to lethal or near-lethal violence as the ultimate assertion of dominance.
  5. Murder-suicide pattern — In some cases, particularly where depression or narcissistic collapse is present, the perpetrator extends the lethal act to themselves, completing what forensic psychiatrists identify as the ultimate expression of control.

Campbell et al.'s (2003) Danger Assessment instrument identified eleven risk factors associated with femicide in abusive relationships, including the perpetrator's access to weapons, prior strangulation attempts, the presence of a child not biologically related to the perpetrator, and the victim's act of leaving or separating. These factors, taken together, constitute a sociological and relational risk profile that is highly predictive — and, crucially, identifiable in advance by trained practitioners.

4.0

Adverse Childhood Experiences and the Intergenerational Transmission of Violence

The relationship between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and adult violent behaviour is among the most robust findings in the behavioural sciences. The landmark ACE Study conducted by Felitti et al. (1998) across a large sample of adults in the United States found strong dose-response relationships between the number of adverse childhood experiences — including physical, emotional, and sexual abuse, neglect, household dysfunction, and exposure to domestic violence — and a wide range of negative adult outcomes, including involvement in violent behaviour both as perpetrators and as victims.

The mechanisms through which childhood adversity elevates adult violence risk are multiple and interacting. At the neurobiological level, chronic early stress disrupts the development of the prefrontal cortex and dysregulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, producing lasting alterations in stress reactivity and impulse control that are examined in detail in Article 4 of this series (van der Kolk, 2014). At the psychological level, childhood exposure to violence shapes the internal working models through which individuals interpret threat, regulate emotion, and conceptualise relationships — producing hypervigilant, avoidant, or disorganised attachment patterns that persist into adult life (Widom, 2014). At the sociological level, children raised in violent households are deprived of the modelling of non-violent conflict resolution, the development of prosocial relationships, and the institutional engagement — with school, community, and civic life — that builds social capital and provides alternative sources of identity and status.

Neurobiological pathway

Chronic early stress disrupts prefrontal cortex development and dysregulates the HPA axis, impairing impulse control and elevating stress reactivity into adulthood.

Psychological pathway

Childhood adversity shapes maladaptive attachment patterns, distorted threat perception, and deficits in emotional regulation that persist across the lifespan.

Social learning pathway

Exposure to violence normalises aggression as a problem-solving strategy and deprives children of models for non-violent conflict resolution.

Social capital pathway

Household dysfunction reduces engagement with school, community, and civic institutions that build prosocial identity and provide alternative sources of status.

Widom's (1989) prospective longitudinal study of abused and neglected children found that childhood victimisation significantly increased the probability of arrest for violent crime in adulthood, even after controlling for sociodemographic variables. Crucially, however, the majority of children who experienced abuse and neglect did not go on to commit violent offences. This finding underlines the probabilistic rather than deterministic nature of the relationship — adverse childhood experiences elevate risk without predetermining outcome, and protective factors including stable relationships with non-abusive adults, educational engagement, and access to mental health support can substantially moderate the trajectory.

5.0

Social Learning, Alienation and the Normalisation of Violence

Bandura's (1977) social learning theory provides a further sociological mechanism through which violent behaviour is acquired and maintained. The theory holds that behaviour, including aggressive behaviour, is learned through observation, imitation, and reinforcement — that individuals acquire behavioural repertoires by observing the behaviour of others, particularly significant others, and internalising the norms and values that those behaviours express. In environments where violence is frequent, normatively unchallenged, and instrumentally effective, it is learned as a legitimate and functional response to frustration, conflict, and perceived disrespect.

The criminological application of social learning theory is well established. Akers (1998) developed the theory into a comprehensive framework identifying four mechanisms through which criminal behaviour is learned: differential association (exposure to criminal definitions and models), definitions (the attitudes and values favouring criminal behaviour), differential reinforcement (the balance of rewards and punishments associated with criminal acts), and imitation (the modelling of criminal behaviour observed in others). Communities characterised by high rates of violence, weak institutional oversight, and strong subcultural norms favouring physical retaliation provide precisely the social learning environment in which these mechanisms are most operative.

Social alienation — the experience of estrangement from the dominant social order, its values, and its institutions — compounds the social learning dynamic by reducing the individual's investment in prosocial norms and the social bonds that ordinarily constrain behaviour. Hirschi's (1969) social control theory proposed that individuals refrain from criminal behaviour not primarily because of fear of punishment but because of their attachment to conventional social bonds — to family, school, employment, and community. When those bonds are weakened or severed by poverty, discrimination, institutional failure, or social marginalisation, the social cost of criminal behaviour is reduced and the threshold for its occurrence is correspondingly lowered.

6.0

The Structural Foundations of Arson

While the psychological function of arson was examined in the preceding article, the sociological conditions that produce fire-setters are equally significant. Arson rates are consistently elevated in communities characterised by high unemployment, concentrated poverty, social disorganisation, and weak community cohesion (Karter, 2012). This association is not coincidental. Communities in which legitimate avenues for economic participation, social recognition, and the exercise of agency are systematically blocked produce individuals for whom the spectacular, transgressive act of fire-setting constitutes a form of visible protest, a claim to power, or an expression of accumulated grievance that no legitimate channel will accommodate.

The Structural Arsonist Profile

Research consistently identifies the typical non-financially motivated arsonist as a young male from a socially marginalised background, with limited educational attainment, poor employment prospects, a history of social rejection or institutional failure, and a felt sense of invisibility and powerlessness within his social environment. The fire represents, in this context, the one act he can commit that the world cannot ignore — a desperate assertion of presence in a system that has rendered him absent.

7.0

Structural Disadvantage, Individual Vulnerability and the Preventive Implication

The sociological forces examined in this article — structural strain, domestic coercive control, adverse childhood experiences, social learning in violent environments, and the alienation produced by systematic exclusion — do not act in isolation from the biological and psychological dimensions identified in the preceding articles. They interact with them, activate them, and in many cases are partially constituted by them. The child whose neurobiological development is disrupted by early trauma enters the social learning environment already compromised. The individual whose psychological architecture is shaped by personality pathology interprets structural frustration through that distorting lens. The relationship between structure and individual, between society and biology, is not sequential but recursive.

This recursiveness has a critically important preventive implication. Because structural conditions and individual vulnerabilities interact and amplify each other, effective prevention does not require addressing the entire system simultaneously. Intervention at any point in the recursive loop can reduce the probability of the convergence that produces extreme violence. The provision of economic support during acute financial crisis removes a structural trigger. The clinical identification and treatment of a coercive control pattern before it escalates removes a relational pathway to lethality. The delivery of trauma-informed support to children exposed to household violence interrupts the intergenerational transmission of adversity. Each of these interventions is feasible, evidence-based, and capable of reducing violence risk without requiring the elimination of structural inequality as a precondition (Farrington, 2003; Widom, 2014).

8.0

Conclusion

Society does not produce violent criminals by design. It produces conditions — through the unequal distribution of opportunity, the tolerance of coercive intimate relationships, the neglect of children exposed to adversity, and the social learning environments of marginalised communities — in which the probability of violent behaviour is systematically elevated for those who already carry biological and psychological vulnerability. The sociological dimension of the biopsychosocial framework does not assign blame to social structure alone, nor does it excuse the individual who acts destructively within that structure. It insists, rather, that any account of severe violent crime that ignores the structural conditions of its occurrence is analytically incomplete and preventively impotent.

The following article, Article 4, descends from the social to the biological, examining the neurological and neurochemical foundations of violence risk. It will show that many of the biological vulnerabilities identified by neuro-criminology are themselves partially produced by the sociological conditions examined here — confirming, once again, the recursive and interactive character of the forces that converge to produce extreme destructive acts.
